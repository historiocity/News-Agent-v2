October prices rose 0.4%, more than forecast, and a December rate hike is now the base case; Treasury’s own data put the 10-year at 4.98%, not 5%

Macro & markets US debt & fiscal New subject Primary source

Independent accounts compared: 3 newsrooms (CNBC, CBS News, BBC), plus Treasury’s Daily Par Yield Curve. The BLS release itself refused our automated request.

The consumer price index rose 0.4% in October, against the 0.3% economists expected and down from 0.6% in September. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, rose 0.3% for the third month in a row; economists had expected 0.2% (CNBC). The mix changed. Energy rose only 0.7% after 5.2% in September, while food rose 0.5% after a flat month, and shelter accounted for more than 30% of the monthly increase. There is no year-over-year figure this month, because the 2025 shutdown left BLS without October 2025 data (CBS News; BBC). The national gas average fell below $4 on Tuesday for the first time in nearly four months, according to AAA as cited by CBS.

The rate path moved. Before the release, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said in London that if inflation came in hot, “a 50 basis point hike in the policy rate should be considered at the December meeting,” adding that “hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk.” After the print, CME’s FedWatch showed an 85% chance of a quarter-point hike in December and 15% for a half-point, CBS reports. Goldman Sachs Asset Management’s Lindsay Rosner called a December hike “our firm base case” and said further hikes in 2027 can’t be ruled out. A weak $42 billion 10-year auction added to the selloff: dealers were left with 10% of it, the most since May, according to One Point BFG’s Peter Boockvar. Producer prices come out on Friday, Nov. 13.

In the series of record. Treasury’s Daily Par Yield Curve for Tuesday shows the 2-year at 4.73% (up from 4.56% on Monday), the 10-year at 4.98% (from 4.88%) and the 30-year at 5.35% (from 5.26%). Why it matters to you: the 10-year sets the price of mortgages and car loans, and a Fed hike feeds straight into credit-card rates. This is also the first inflation report since an election fought over living costs, and CNBC reports the White House plans to keep its tariffs despite that backlash.

Divergence — did the 10-year hit 5%?

What is in dispute. A dispute over a number. CNBC’s headline says the 10-year “races back to 5%,” and its text says it “traded above 5% before paring gains” and was last at 4.992%. Treasury’s end-of-day par yield is 4.98%. Separately, Waller is arguing for a half-point hike while futures markets price mostly a quarter-point.

Why the vantage differs. CNBC reports live trading quotes from a vendor, which capture intraday highs. Treasury publishes one closing estimate from its own curve model. Waller is one voting governor trying to protect the Fed’s credibility; futures traders are pricing the whole committee.

What it means for the reader. Under your standing rule, the Treasury figure is the one this brief prints: the 10-year did not close at 5% on Tuesday. The decision that changes your borrowing costs is December’s, and the size of the hike is still open.

The House votes 211–204 to shelve the Hegseth impeachment; Democrats say they will be back in January

US politics & policy Broader domestic institutions Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 (NBC News, CBS News, Fox News, and X’s news summary of the absences), plus the House Clerk’s party counts. Trigger (threshold event): the forced vote happened Tuesday. Yesterday’s lead covered only the deadline for it.

The House voted 211–204 on Tuesday evening to refer Rep. Thomas Massie’s eight articles against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth to the Judiciary Committee, which kills them in this Congress. Massie was the only Republican to vote with Democrats to keep the resolution alive (NBC News; CBS News). “I wasn’t surprised, but I was disappointed,” Massie said. He says he won’t file again but will force a war-powers vote. Even Republican critics of Hegseth voted no. Rep. Don Bacon said he might feel differently if the articles covered “firing 80 generals,” and Rep. Darrell Issa said it would “die in the Senate” anyway. Trump called the result an “EXONERATION.” Hegseth, at a morning briefing, called it “a farce.” Before that briefing, Pentagon officials told reporters they would be “physically removed” if they “get out of line,” according to journalists present (NBC News).

The absences. The House Clerk lists 214 Democrats. With Massie among the 204 votes against referral, at most 203 Democrats voted no, so at least 11 did not. A switch of four votes would have changed the result. “11 Democrats” trended on X this morning, where users named Nancy Pelosi and Melanie Stansbury among those absent. We could not confirm who missed the vote. Why: the Clerk’s roll-call page did not load for us, and the names come only from posts on X. Democrats are promising more. Jeffries said, “This is not the end of it. It’s just the beginning,” and Schumer said the Senate should “put Pete Hegseth on trial” (Fox News). Why it matters to you: this was the first recorded House vote on the Iran war since the election. Democrats now have a target for January, when they take both chambers.

Divergence — is this about crimes or about the war?

What is in dispute. A dispute over law. Massie says Hegseth broke the War Powers Resolution and the laws of war. Hegseth says the actions were “lawful” and the charges are “partisan policy disagreements.” Bacon and Issa object to the war’s handling but say it doesn’t amount to “high crimes and misdemeanors.”

Why the vantage differs. Massie lost a primary in which Trump and Hegseth backed his opponent, and Issa says that is his motive. Bacon and Issa are both leaving Congress. Jeffries and Schumer gain from keeping the war in the news before they take over. Trump called the outcome an exoneration, but referral to committee is not a ruling on the merits.

What it means for the reader. The vote shows that Republicans won’t remove Hegseth over the war. Whether he is removed now depends on what Democrats do with their majorities in January. The war-powers vote Massie promised will be the next recorded test.

AI & tech industry International with US channel Continuation Independent corroboration

House Foreign Affairs chairman Brian Mast told ABC News he is prepared to subpoena Jensen Huang if he doesn’t answer by Wednesday a request from Mast and two other chairmen, John Moolenaar (China committee) and Rick Crawford (Intelligence), for a closed-door briefing on Nvidia’s protections against foreign espionage. Any House GOP subpoena would likely lapse when Democrats take over the committees in January. Republican Sen. The chips. Reuters found an Oct. 21 procurement record showing that Northwestern Polytechnical University in Xi’an, on the U.S. Entity List since 2001 and one of China’s “Seven Sons of National Defense,” bought two H200 chips for 874,700 yuan ($123,000) through a local reseller, not from Nvidia. Reuters reviewed 26 Chinese records this year showing restricted Nvidia chips or rented Nvidia computing going to universities and research institutes, several with defense links (Reuters via Al Jazeera). The administration’s January policy allows some H200 sales to China case by case, but not to Entity List institutions. NBC News reports that the Justice Department asked Commerce to pause its part of a probe into whether an Nvidia cloud customer smuggled chips to China, after an Nvidia representative contacted Commerce investigators. We could not confirm that account. Why: NBC cites one unnamed person, and we could read only the opening of its story. Nvidia’s board has hired Wachtell Lipton and former acting Manhattan U.S. Attorney Joon Kim to run its own investigation (CNBC). Why it matters to you: this is turning from a scandal about one man into a question of whether export controls on AI chips work at all.

What is in dispute. A dispute over the outlook. Cramer, and Larry Kudlow on Fox Business, say Huang should step down. Nvidia’s board says it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised, and Trump has called Huang “an honourable guy.” An NBC News analysis argues he “just might survive.”

Why the vantage differs. Cramer sits on Senate Banking, which oversees export-control law, and Kudlow speaks to investors. The board answers to shareholders who have lost value since the reports. Trump has made chip sales part of his China dealings. The Reuters procurement record is a document, and none of these people’s opinions are.

What it means for the reader. Watch two things: whether Huang agrees to brief or is subpoenaed, and whether Commerce acts on the Northwestern Polytechnical purchase. A smuggling enforcement action would affect the whole AI supply chain, not only Huang’s job.

International with US channel Energy & grid Continuation Primary source

Independent accounts compared: 3 (NBC News, Al Jazeera, CNBC), plus the Justice Department’s release. Trigger (new verifiable fact): an unsealed superseding indictment. Yesterday’s Iran lead noted the Mildenhall threat without any charges.

An eight-count superseding indictment unsealed Tuesday in Manhattan charges Mohammad Baqer Saad Dawood al-Saadi, Mohsen Mohajeri and Mohammad Ali al-Hassani with terrorism offenses on behalf of Iran’s Revolutionary Guard and Kata’ib Hizballah. The charges cover nearly 20 attacks and attempted attacks in Europe and the U.S., plus the September plot at RAF Fairford, the British base then housing U.S. bombers (Justice Department). Prosecutors say al-Hassani paid for surveillance of the base on Aug. 22. They say he then had associates buy vans, fuel barrels and metal objects that could serve as shrapnel, and research potassium chloride, with decals to make the vans look like fuel-delivery and pool-supply trucks. On Sept. 26, the indictment says, he directed three vans toward the base, and at least two were turned back at a security roadblock. Al-Saadi allegedly helped coordinate from a New York jail through coded calls to relatives in Iraq. Only al-Saadi is in custody (NBC News; Al Jazeera). The charges are allegations, and al-Saadi pleaded not guilty to the earlier counts in June.

The threat at home. The New York Times reports that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who runs the “Operation Economic Outcast” sanctions campaign, was moved from his Washington home to a military base after officials concluded Iranian plotting against U.S. officials had reached a more advanced stage (CNBC). We could not confirm how serious the threat is. Why: Reuters says that was not immediately clear, and no official has described it on the record. Iran’s security chief Mohsen Rezaei says Hormuz will stay closed until Washington meets seven conditions, including lifting sanctions and the blockade, and calls talk of negotiations “a pure lie.” The IRGC says three tankers hit mines Tuesday, and monitors count at least 37 attacks on ships near the strait so far in November, the most of any month in the war (Al Jazeera). Brent was about $92 in Asian trading. Why it matters to you: U.S. Central Command runs the war from MacDill Air Force Base in Tampa, and the shipping attacks are what keeps fuel prices high.

Divergence — is the war winding down?

What is in dispute. A dispute over the outlook. Rubio says “there is no need for major combat operations” because the economic campaign is “so devastating.” Trump sees “positive momentum.” Iran says there are no talks, the strait stays shut, and attacks on ships are at a wartime high. U.S. officials say Iran is escalating plots against Americans.

Why the vantage differs. The administration is selling sanctions and the blockade as working, a week after losing an election in which the war was an issue. Rezaei wants concessions before any public talks. Prosecutors’ account comes from an indictment, which is one side’s case until trial.

What it means for the reader. Less bombing doesn’t mean less risk. Iran’s pressure has moved from the battlefield to shipping lanes and to plots against people. Hormuz reopening is the signal that matters for prices.

A PAHO panel meets through Friday to decide whether measles is endemic in the U.S. again; Florida is behind on vaccination and proposing to drop four school vaccines

Science & public health Florida & Tampa Bay New subject Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 5 (CNBC, Scientific American, CIDRAP, ABC News, Fortune), plus PAHO’s own notices and the Florida Department of Health.

PAHO’s measles verification commission, an independent technical body, is meeting behind closed doors from Tuesday through Friday, Nov. 13. It will assess whether the same measles strain has circulated in the U.S. for 12 months or more, counting from the West Texas outbreak that began on Jan. 20, 2025 (PAHO). Its conclusions go to PAHO’s director; if it finds endemic spread, the U.S. loses the elimination status it gained in 2000. Canada lost its status last year. The CDC has confirmed 5,182 cases so far in 2026, compared with 2,289 in 2025 and 285 in 2024 (Scientific American). Pennsylvania alone has passed 1,500 cases, 97 of them in pregnant women (ABC News). A Johns Hopkins tool puts this year’s outbreaks at about $83 million in public-health response, lost work and medical costs (CNBC). CIDRAP notes the outcome could depend on genetic sequencing. If the U.S. outbreaks are not linked to one another, the U.S. could keep its status.

Florida. Fortune reports 162 Florida cases as of Oct. 17, many tied to an outbreak at Ave Maria University near Naples. It also reports that only 88.9% of Florida kindergartners had the MMR vaccine in 2025–26, below the 92.4% national average and the 95% needed for herd immunity, and that the state has no measles dashboard (Fortune). The Department of Health is proposing to drop hepatitis B, chickenpox, Hib and pneumococcal vaccines from school-entry requirements. MMR stays required because it is in state law, and the Legislature did not take up Surgeon General Ladapo’s call to repeal it. Why it matters to you: a loss of status would be official confirmation that measles is spreading continuously in the U.S., and Florida’s vaccination rates are below the national average.

Divergence — how many have died?

What is in dispute. A dispute over the count. States have confirmed eight measles-linked deaths this year (six in Pennsylvania, one in New York, one in Wisconsin), according to CNBC. Scientific American says the CDC has recorded four. Florida’s own case count is also hard to pin down: Fortune says public trackers disagree on the statewide total.

Why the vantage differs. State health departments report deaths as they confirm them. The CDC’s national count has run behind state confirmations, and neither source explains the gap. Florida removed county-level disease data from its website in May; the data has since returned, but there is no measles dashboard.

What it means for the reader. Treat the lower federal figures as a minimum. The decision that matters this week is PAHO’s. The one that matters locally is whether Florida finalizes its vaccine-rule change.

HUD finalizes its rule ending housing aid for mixed-status families and requiring every tenant, at any age, to prove citizenship or status

US politics & policy Broader domestic institutions New subject Primary source

Independent accounts compared: 2 (NAHRO, the public-housing directors’ trade group; and a Bloomberg Law summary), plus the final rule and HUD’s release. Underreported: none of the general-news fronts we swept carried it.

The final rule, published tomorrow (Federal Register 2026-23003), takes effect Monday, Dec. 14. It does two things in public housing, Section 8 vouchers and project-based rental aid. First, every family member, “regardless of age,” must provide evidence of citizenship or eligible immigration status, including people 62 and older, a group current rules do not fully cover. Second, “prorated” assistance, which lets a family with one ineligible member keep a reduced subsidy for the others, becomes temporary instead of indefinite. Housing agencies must notify tenants by Monday, Jan. 11, 2027. Mixed families then have 90 days after Dec. 14 to submit evidence, and other tenants must do so at their next recertification. Agencies can defer termination for up to 18 months.

The numbers in the rule. HUD counts 19,810 mixed families eligible for that deferral and expects at most about 5% to contest eviction. It applies a 1.05% documentation-gap rate to about 1.85 million existing citizen tenants who have never shown proof, which implies roughly 19,000 citizens could have trouble producing papers. HUD estimates about 16,000 of the affected families, roughly 78%, would leave assisted housing together, and that $167–218 million a year in rental aid would shift to fully eligible families on waiting lists. Commenters quoted in the rule cite a HUD estimate that $311–385 million more would be needed to keep current capacity. HUD says a HUD–DHS audit found nearly 200,000 tenants with incomplete or unknown eligibility, and that its housing serves only a quarter of eligible households. NAHRO and Bloomberg Law have covered it, and we found no general-news coverage. We found no Florida or Tampa Bay breakdown of affected families. Why it matters to you: local housing authorities in Pasco, Pinellas and Hillsborough have to start notifying tenants within weeks, and the rule reaches every household member, not only those whose status is in question.

Divergence — who loses their home?

What is in dispute. A dispute over the effects. HUD says the rule moves aid from ineligible people to “fully eligible households currently awaiting assistance” and calls its net effect on homelessness “ambiguous.” Commenters quoted in the rule say mixed families already pay higher rents for smaller subsidies, so pushing them out raises per-household costs, and that citizens without papers, including elderly tenants, could lose aid.

Why the vantage differs. HUD is carrying out an administration immigration priority and counts waiting-list families as the beneficiaries. Housing agencies, represented by NAHRO, bear the paperwork and lose rent revenue. Tenant advocates speak for the families. HUD itself says the falling count of mixed families since 2019 may reflect less reporting rather than a real decline.

What it means for the reader. The HUD figures are estimates, not results. The first real counts will come when housing agencies send their January notices. A lawsuit before Dec. 14 is the likeliest early turn.

Under what conditions should we keep following HUD’s mixed-status rule?
