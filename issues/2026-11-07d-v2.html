Poland sets national mourning for Nov. 8–9 after the Kraków attack

International / NATO ally Follow-up Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 5. Trigger: President Nawrocki signs national mourning; more victim IDs.

President Karol Nawrocki on Saturday night signed a regulation declaring Nov. 8 and 9 national days of mourning after a gunman killed six people on Kraków’s Main Market Square, PAP reported; Prime Minister Donald Tusk said his office would set the form. BBC, The Guardian, and AP hold the square facts steady: six dead (four women, two men — three Polish, one British, two still unnamed), 16 injured including five foreign nationals and two police officers, one critically. Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński again called the 25-year-old suspect’s views “ultra-nationalistic”; three bodies, likely family, were found at his home ~200 km away. Kraków’s own mourning runs through Nov. 11.

US channel: Poland is a frontline NATO ally; UK PM Andy Burnham and Foreign Secretary Ed Miliband issued solidarity statements; the Foreign Office updated travel advice. No US nationals named among the dead at press time.

What to watch: charging document; any second arrest among “supporters” Tusk vowed to detain; identification of the two unnamed victims.

Divergence — terrorism label vs. lone radicalised shooter

What is in dispute. Significance dispute: Polish officials (Tusk, defence, interior) call it terrorism; early international copy also frames a lone far-right radical with family corpses at home. Square facts (six dead, car firefight, military kit) are not contested.

Why the vantage differs. Polish officials need a legal and political frame for national mourning and any follow-on detentions. Wires lead with casualty and ideology colour. Timing: European desks moved faster than US evening wraps.

What it means for the reader. Treat the attack as confirmed mass killing with a far-right tag from Polish authorities, not yet a proven conspiracy. The observation that settles the frame is the charging document and any second arrest.

Mississippi and Alaska harden into affidavit fights and a rejected-absentee lawsuit

Elections Follow-up Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4. Trigger: NRSC/Hyde-Smith preserve affidavits; Peltola suit over rejected absentees.

Neither remaining Senate race changes chamber control (Democrats already at 54–44), but both now have litigation layers. In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom leads Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,574 votes (467,930–465,356) with ~97% counted, per WAPT and Fox; mail ballots can arrive through Nov. 10. USA Today reports Hyde-Smith’s campaign and the NRSC asked all 82 counties to set affidavit ballots aside and preserve them — the usual prelude to court — while Colom’s counsel says pulling already-processed ballots would itself violate state law. Mississippi has no recount statute for this race.

In Alaska, Mary Peltola leads Sen. Dan Sullivan by about 637 votes with roughly three-quarters counted. On Nov. 6 her supporting group sued the Division of Elections for names of voters whose absentees were rejected; the state refused, saying the records are preliminary (The Hill). Absentees can arrive through Nov. 18, when ranked-choice tabulation is also due. DOJ’s pre-election bid to block certification if Sullivan lost was dismissed; the department vowed to appeal.

Divergence — ballot preservation as integrity vs. as delay

What is in dispute. Facts dispute on process: Republicans cast affidavit preservation as election-integrity housekeeping; Democrats cast it as an attempt to slow or challenge ballots already in the count. Vote margins themselves are not seriously contested across desks.

Why the vantage differs. Party lawyers are building records for county-by-county challenges (Mississippi) or rejected-ballot cures (Alaska). Access differs: campaigns see partial county dumps; wire desks see statewide AP tallies.

What it means for the reader. Believe the published margins until Nov. 10 (Mississippi) and Nov. 18 (Alaska). The observation that settles the fights is which affidavits/absentees are accepted and whether any injunction actually stops a count.

Continuing thread: election certification and the DOJ.

Herzog will hand Eisenkot the mandate on Sunday

International / US ally Follow-up Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 3. Trigger: Saturday decision; formal Sunday ceremony.

Israeli President Isaac Herzog told Yashar leader Gadi Eisenkot on Saturday evening that he would form the next government, passing over Benjamin Netanyahu, and will formally hand him the mandate at a Sunday ceremony, Al Jazeera and The Washington Post report. Some 67 lawmakers endorsed Eisenkot versus 46 for Netanyahu. Eisenkot has 28 days (plus a possible 14-day extension) to build a 61-seat coalition. His four-party alliance holds 48 seats; Netanyahu’s bloc holds 53; Arab-led parties hold a record 19. Eisenkot has ruled out a formal coalition with Arab-led parties, leaving ultra-Orthodox partners or a minority government as the live paths. The Jerusalem Post likewise frames Sunday’s handoff as the start of the clock.

US channel: Israel remains the central US ally in the Iran war theatre; a post-Netanyahu cabinet would reshape Washington’s interlocutor set for any Hormuz diplomacy.

Google commits $17B to a Cheyenne AI campus built around 702 MW of fuel cells

Data centers / power / AI New subject Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4. Promoted from Also noted after primary docs opened.

Google announced a $17 billion first Wyoming data-center campus in Laramie County, paired with a first-of-its-kind commercial-scale 702 MW Bloom Energy fuel-cell plant under a 20-year PPA with American Electric Power (~$4B AEP investment; full operation targeted end-2028). Power also comes from Tallgrass and Black Hills gas generation; Google pledges to pay 100% of its electricity and use air cooling to cut water strain. Data Center Dynamics notes the site was acquired from Crusoe (Project Jade/Tembo) and that the oft-cited 2.7 GW figure is not confirmed IT load. Carbon capture to Tallgrass’s Eastern Wyoming hub is promised at “a large majority” of fuel-cell emissions — an engineering claim, not yet an operating result.

Divergence — campus size vs. IT load

What is in dispute. Facts dispute: some secondary copy treats “2.7 GW” as the data center’s computing capacity; Google has not confirmed IT load, and utility filings describe a broader power-resource mix.

Why the vantage differs. Trade press recycles earlier Project Jade figures; Google’s own blog sticks to dollar investment, fuel-cell megawatts, and community taxes. Access: only Google and AEP have the PPA text.

What it means for the reader. Believe the $17B and 702 MW fuel-cell commitments; treat 2.7 GW as unconfirmed until Google publishes an engineering breakdown. The observation that settles it is a disclosed IT-load figure or utility tariff filing.
