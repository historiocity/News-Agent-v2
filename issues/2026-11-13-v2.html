Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia’s chief executive on Thursday; a Republican Senate chairman has asked him and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify in December

AI & tech industry US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 6 newsrooms (CBS News, NPR, CNBC, the Guardian, NBC News and Fortune), plus Bloomberg Law seen through a search excerpt. Trigger (threshold event): Huang’s resignation and the Senate Banking Committee’s letters, both since yesterday’s lead on the board meeting.

Nvidia’s board announced Thursday that Huang had resigned effective immediately. Chief financial officer Colette Kress becomes interim CEO and keeps the CFO job, and Harvey Jones was named the company’s first board chair (CBS News). The board says it will search inside and outside the company, and Nvidia still plans to report quarterly earnings on Tuesday, Nov. 17 (NPR). After the announcement, Reuters reported that Huang had also had a relationship with a second Nvidia employee, as CBS relays. Senate Banking Chairman Tim Scott, a South Carolina Republican, sent letters Thursday evening asking Huang and Lutnick to testify, and warned that the committee is “prepared to use all tools at our disposal, including subpoenas” (Fortune). Lutnick’s spokeswoman says he “proactively agreed” to appear (CNBC). Rep. Brian Mast, R-Fla., says Huang has also agreed to brief House members on his relationship with the alleged spy (NBC News).

The pressure is now on Commerce. The Guardian, citing the Associated Press, reports that White House officials are discussing whether to fire Lutnick, after it emerged that an intelligence report on Huang’s relationship had circulated among senior officials and that Commerce had delayed blacklisting Megaspeed, the Singapore company Alice Huang worked for. CNBC says Megaspeed bought more than $4.8 billion of advanced Nvidia chips over two years. Republican Sens. John Cornyn (“Heads should roll”), John Curtis and Jim Banks have criticised Lutnick, and so have Democrats. We could not confirm that a firing is under active consideration. Why: the AP report rests on unnamed officials and we saw it only as the Guardian relayed it; the White House has not commented.

Why it matters to you. This has become a fight over how the U.S. polices AI chip exports, and it lands on the Commerce Department, which writes those rules. NBC reports that a loophole letting Chinese firms rent top Nvidia chips through overseas cloud data centers “remains wide open” (NBC News). Elizabeth Warren, who takes over the Banking Committee in January, says Huang’s departure does not absolve him or the company (CBS). For anyone advising banks or companies on AI build-outs, a stricter Commerce regime on where chips can be used is now likelier than it was a week ago.

Divergence — is this Huang’s scandal or Commerce’s?

What is in dispute. A dispute over who is accountable, plus a factual gap on dates. Banks says Lutnick “appears to have used loopholes to allow some of these chip sales to occur”; Mast says Congress must investigate Commerce but that he has confidence in Lutnick (CNBC). CNBC and Bloomberg Law say Huang was asked for Thursday, Dec. 3 and Lutnick for Thursday, Dec. 10; Fortune and the Washington Examiner give Dec. 3 for both. We could not confirm Lutnick’s date, because the letters have not been published in full.

Why the vantage differs. Banks, Cornyn and Curtis are Republicans pressing for tighter China controls, and they gain by putting distance between the party and the scandal before January. Mast is a Florida Republican and a Trump ally. Warren and Schumer want the administration’s knowledge on the record before Democrats take the gavels. Lutnick’s office is defending itself, and its voluntary appearance gets ahead of a subpoena.

What it means for the reader. Watch whether Commerce adds Megaspeed to the Entity List or closes the cloud-rental gap before Dec. 3. Either would show the administration moving the blame onto rules rather than people.

A Microsoft 365 outage that began Thursday afternoon was still disrupting Outlook, Teams and Copilot for millions on Friday morning; Microsoft rules out a cyberattack

AI & tech industry AI and financial services New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 5 (CNBC, BBC, the Guardian, NPR and Fox Business), each quoting Microsoft’s own status updates.

Connection problems began shortly before 1 p.m. ET on Thursday and ran through the Asian business day and into the European and early U.S. morning, hitting Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and Copilot (CNBC). In a 7 a.m. ET update Microsoft said it was making “encouraging recovery progress in Europe,” that North America was “improving,” and that it had extended the window for delayed email to 48 hours so messages are not lost. Downdetector logged about 140,000 Outlook reports from 74 countries on Thursday evening, and OpenAI and Anthropic both reported knock-on problems (BBC). The U.S. House, courts from Los Angeles to Texas, Japan’s government and Hong Kong bankers were among those affected (NPR). Microsoft has more than 430 million 365 users and says over 90% of the Fortune 500 use Copilot (Guardian). We could not confirm when service will be fully restored. Why: Microsoft has given no time estimate and says restarted servers can fail again once traffic returns.

Why it matters to you. If your clients or your own office run on 365, Thursday afternoon’s mail may arrive late or out of order, so check anything time-sensitive sent since about 1 p.m. ET Thursday. It is also a test case for the AI pitch in financial services: Copilot went down with email, and the AI labs that rely on Microsoft were hit too. The post-incident report Microsoft has promised is the document to read.

Divergence — what actually broke?

What is in dispute. A dispute over cause. Microsoft first blamed “elevated load,” and CNBC describes an initial traffic surge. Microsoft’s admin alerts, quoted by Fox Business, say the load was not driven by more traffic but by servers that were “unexpectedly unavailable,” failing automated health checks and leaving the rest overloaded; the Guardian says Microsoft has pointed to settings applied to servers. A cybersecurity lawyer on Fox Business says it is reasonable to wonder whether AI or hackers were involved; Microsoft says it sees no malicious activity.

Why the vantage differs. Microsoft controls all the evidence and has a commercial reason to frame this as a capacity problem that is being fixed. Outside commentators have no access to the logs and fill the gap with the scenarios their audiences fear most. The newsrooms are all relaying Microsoft’s status page at different hours, which explains much of the drift.

What it means for the reader. Until the root-cause report appears, treat “not a cyberattack” as Microsoft’s statement, not a finding, and treat a configuration error as the leading explanation.

Dig deeper: Microsoft’s own account, in order

Thursday: “elevated load across multiple regions” from a “number of servers being unexpectedly unavailable.” By 6:30 p.m. ET: “actions taken thus far have not provided the anticipated relief.” Later: a “core process on affected servers is becoming overwhelmed”; engineers isolated some infrastructure, limited traffic types in some locations and rebuilt servers on a previous configuration to test whether that stopped them hanging (Fox Business). Friday: “early signs of stabilisation, but service capacity has not yet returned to normal levels” (Guardian), then recovery “improving” in North America (CNBC). Microsoft says a post-incident report will include “defence-in-depth repair items.”

Iran’s Revolutionary Guard says it destroyed a U.S. early-warning radar in Bahrain, its first claimed hit on U.S. forces in the Gulf since September, hours after Trump said “we don’t need a deal”

International with US channel Energy & grid Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 read in full or in substantial excerpt (Al Jazeera, which co-bylines Reuters; the Guardian; Radio Free Europe/Radio Liberty; Rudaw), plus AP seen through a search excerpt. All rely on Iranian state media for the claim itself. Trigger (new verifiable fact): the Guard’s claimed strike and Trump’s statement ending the push for a deal, both since yesterday’s lead on Israel’s strike preparations.

The Guard said its aerospace force “targeted and destroyed” a U.S. early-warning radar in Bahrain, home of the Navy’s Fifth Fleet, in retaliation for what it called an air attack on a telecommunications tower in Sirik, a port town on the Strait of Hormuz (Al Jazeera). It warned that if “violations” recur it is ready “to engage without limits.” The Guard’s statement did not directly blame the United States for the Sirik strike, and neither Washington nor Manama commented at first (RFE/RL). AP adds that the Guard also says a tanker hit a mine in the strait. We could not confirm that any radar was hit or that a tanker struck a mine. Why: both claims come only from Iranian state media, and no U.S., Bahraini or independent imagery had appeared when we published.

On Thursday Trump wrote, “We don’t need a deal with Iran. Those days have long since passed. We’ve got their money, Hundreds of Billions of Dollars, and we’ve got their Strait!” and told Vice President Vance, who had been talking almost daily with Qatari and Pakistani mediators, to focus on “Democrat run Cities and States” instead (Guardian). Asked by Hugh Hewitt whether he would use force against Iranian threats, Trump said, “No hesitation,” and that Iran’s government outlasting his presidency “would be a defeat” (Rudaw).

Why it matters to you. Central Command, which runs this war, is headquartered at MacDill Air Force Base in Tampa. Oil has eased as Gulf exports recover (see the IEA item below), and a new exchange of fire in the strait is the quickest way to reverse that. Taking Vance off the file also removes the official most identified with a negotiated end.

Divergence — is the war winding down or heating up?

What is in dispute. A dispute over facts on the ground and over intent. Iran says it hit a U.S. radar; the U.S. has not confirmed it. Trump says the U.S. has won and needs no deal; Secretary of State Marco Rubio says economic pressure is working well enough that big strikes are unnecessary (Al Jazeera); Iran’s chief negotiator, Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf, says Tehran is waiting on Washington’s answer to seven conditions and that “the ball is now in the American court” (Rudaw). Israel’s public broadcaster KAN reported that CENTCOM told Israel to prepare for renewed operations; a CENTCOM official called that “pure speculation.”

Why the vantage differs. The Guard’s claims are meant to deter and are aimed partly at a domestic audience. The Pentagon rarely confirms damage quickly. Trump is declaring victory while keeping the threat of force; Rubio speaks for the sanctions track; Ghalibaf wants the U.S. to look like the side refusing talks. Each outlet relays these through Iranian state media, Israeli media or U.S. officials, and none has independent access to Bahrain or Sirik.

What it means for the reader. The tell is whether the Pentagon confirms damage or casualties. If it does, Trump has said he would not hesitate, and the lull that let Gulf oil flow again would be at risk.

Florida / Tampa Bay International with US channel New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 (Al Jazeera, read in full; Politico, The Hill and Mediaite, seen through search excerpts, Mediaite with a transcript), plus an AP report from Havana carried by ABC News.

“I want to get Cuba done before I leave,” Trump said in a phone interview on Thursday. Hewitt asked, “So, if it took a little bit of military force, a week campaign, you’d be okay with that?” and Trump answered, “I would certainly consider that, yes” (Mediaite transcript). He named Marco Rubio, “because Marco’s parents are from … Cuba,” and said he won the Cuban vote in Florida by 94%. Politico says it is the first time he has put a timeline on a possible operation. Since January the U.S. has kept a de facto oil blockade on the island, after the seizure of Venezuela’s Nicolás Maduro cut off its main fuel supply, and this month added sanctions on 10 people and entities (Al Jazeera). On Monday he said Cuba “will fall fairly soon” (The Hill). In Havana, hundreds line up overnight at charity kitchens for what may be their only meal, in a crisis AP says has been worsened by U.S. energy restrictions (AP via ABC News).

Why it matters to you. Tampa Bay has one of the oldest Cuban communities in the country, and any military move or collapse on the island would show up first in Florida, in a sea migration, Coast Guard interdictions and family remittances. Politically, Trump is tying the idea to Florida’s Cuban-American vote. From January a Democratic Congress can force a war-powers vote.

Divergence — plan or talk?

What is in dispute. A dispute over intent. Trump now says he would consider force and wants it done by January 2029. In March the head of U.S. Southern Command told senators that troops were not rehearsing an invasion, and the White House has announced no military plan (Al Jazeera). Trump frames U.S. help as “humanitarian”; the AP’s reporting from Havana shows the U.S. fuel squeeze deepening the hunger he describes. Cuban officials say the island is ready for any attack.

Why the vantage differs. Trump was speaking to a friendly conservative host the week after his party lost Congress, and Cuba plays well with a Florida base. Southern Command’s statement is eight months old and was given to Congress under oath. AP has reporters on the ground in Havana; the U.S. outlets are working from the interview transcript.

What it means for the reader. Words alone do not move ships. The signal to watch is a change in Southern Command’s posture or a surge at sea in the Florida Straits, not another interview.

Chris Van Hollen will challenge Chuck Schumer for Senate majority leader; Sanders backs him, and Schumer says he has the votes

US politics & policy Broader domestic institutions New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 (CBS News, the Guardian, NPR and ABC News), all read in full.

The Maryland Democrat announced Thursday that “we need new leadership” to answer what he called voters’ demand for change (CBS News). Schumer replied less than a minute later: “55 seats and counting is an amazing result — and I have the votes to continue serving as Democratic Leader” (Guardian). The vote is a closed-door secret ballot in early December, and the winner needs a majority of the caucus. It is the first contested race for Democratic leader in decades. Bernie Sanders backed Van Hollen; incoming senators Abdul El-Sayed of Michigan and Peggy Flanagan of Minnesota have said they oppose Schumer, and Elissa Slotkin says she is “seeking change.” Ruben Gallego and Kirsten Gillibrand are publicly with Schumer (NPR). Van Hollen called himself the underdog on MSNOW (ABC News).

Why it matters to you. From January the majority leader decides what reaches the Senate floor: the chip-export bills now gathering steam after Nvidia, the oversight fights with the White House, any attempt to reverse Medicaid and food-stamp cuts, and Trump’s nominees. Schumer represents New York and its financial industry; Van Hollen is running on “unrig an economy rigged against working people & fueled by political corruption,” which points to tougher fights with banks, crypto and prediction markets.

Divergence — does Schumer have the votes?

What is in dispute. A dispute over a count no one can see. Schumer says he has the votes; CBS reports that some Democrats have not committed to him without endorsing anyone else; NPR describes “growing resistance” inside the caucus; Gillibrand predicts “enormous gratitude” for his work in winning nine seats.

Why the vantage differs. Schumer recruited and raised money for many of the winners and needs to look inevitable. Van Hollen and Sanders need the race to look open so that undecided senators feel safe. Gillibrand chairs the campaign committee that Schumer’s strategy ran through. The ballot is secret, so public pledges may not match the vote.

What it means for the reader. Count named senators, not statements. If several sitting senators beyond Sanders commit to Van Hollen before December, Schumer’s claim is in trouble.

The Justice Department moved to seize $1.2 billion stolen from Binance by North Korean hackers and took down “FaceStealer,” a deepfake video-call scam

AI and financial servicesUS financialsPrimary source

Independent accounts compared: 3 (CBS News and CNBC, read in full; CyberScoop through a search excerpt), plus the Justice Department’s release. Underreported: most coverage leads with the Binance money, not the scam.

The Justice Department says North Korean military hackers it calls TraderTraitor stole more than $6 billion from Binance around Oct. 29; more than $2.7 billion has been frozen, and courts have authorized seizure of more than $1.2 billion of that (CBS News; CNBC). Separately, the FBI seized infrastructure behind FaceStealer, which it says stole more than $400 million in 18 months: hackers hijack a contact’s Telegram, LinkedIn or Slack account, invite the target to a fake Zoom or Teams call, play secretly recorded webcam footage or a deepfake of someone the target knows, claim a technical glitch and get the victim to install malware (CyberScoop). The FBI found more than 900 recorded webcam videos of victims. Why it matters: this is AI-enabled fraud aimed at professionals, and it now reaches software developers as a route into supply-chain attacks.
