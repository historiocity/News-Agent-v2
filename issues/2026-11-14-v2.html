The FAA grounded every departing U.S. flight for more than four hours on Friday; Duffy blames old routers and copper lines and asks Congress for $50 billion

Broader domestic institutions US politics & policy New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 7 newsrooms (NPR, Fox Business, CNBC, BBC, NBC News, Al Jazeera and Fox 13 Tampa Bay), all relying on the FAA’s short notices and the Duffy–Mullin White House briefing. The FAA itself has not briefed reporters.

The FAA ordered the halt at 3:54 p.m. ET “due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety” and lifted it at 8:15 p.m. (NBC News). It is the second nationwide ground stop since Sept. 11, 2001, and the longest (CNBC). At a White House briefing, Duffy said instability in radar and communications at the regional control centers began about 3:36 p.m.: “We have old switches, old routers. We use 1980s little copper lines. It’s an old system that went down and went down everywhere. It got overloaded.” Pressed on why the system overloaded on a normal travel day, he had no ready answer (NPR). Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said “we have seen zero evidence this is a cyber attack.” Air Space Intelligence, which makes the FAA’s new AI-assisted SMART tool, said its product was not the cause (NPR). CNBC counted more than 3,000 U.S. cancellations by 5:45 a.m. Saturday on top of about 9,000 Friday, some of them weather-related.

Why it matters to you. Duffy says the legacy equipment will keep failing without a full rebuild, and he wants $50 billion in emergency money on top of the $12.5 billion already approved (Fox Business). That request now goes to a Congress Democrats take over in January, less than two weeks before Thanksgiving travel. At Tampa International, 156 of 536 flights were delayed and 96 cancelled before the stop was lifted, and the airport warned of knock-on delays (Fox 13). Sen. Richard Blumenthal has called for a “prompt, penetrating investigation.” We could not confirm the technical cause. Why: the only account is Duffy’s, the FAA has published nothing beyond its notices, and Mullin, in remarks quoted by Fox 13, said “we’re still looking.”

Divergence — how big, and how sure are they?

What is in dispute. The size of the disruption and how settled the cause is. Friday’s cancellation count ranges from about 4,700 (Al Jazeera, citing Flightradar24) to nearly 8,000 (BBC, citing FlightAware) and about 9,000 (CNBC, also FlightAware, including weather). Mullin told the briefing there was “zero evidence” of an attack, but in remarks reported by Fox 13 he added that “we’re still looking.”

Why the vantage differs. The trackers count at different hours and some include weather. Duffy has an interest in framing this as old equipment, because that supports his $50 billion request; Mullin’s department owns the cyber question and is hedging until it has finished looking. Airlines have long blamed the FAA for delays, and air-traffic failures generally do not oblige them to pay compensation.

What it means for the reader. Treat the numbers as ranges. The thing to watch is whether the FAA publishes its own findings, and whether Congress attaches the money to a specific contract and timeline or to a general fund.

Trump will meet Xi and Putin together in Shenzhen on Thursday, the first meeting of its kind; the White House says nuclear arms and Iran are on the agenda and Ukraine is not

Geopolitics International with US channel New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 5 (BBC, CBS News, NBC News, ABC News and Al Jazeera), all from the same White House statement and background call with senior officials. Moscow and Beijing had not confirmed details when the BBC published.

White House spokeswoman Olivia Wales said Trump will “conclude his visit to China with a trilateral meeting” with Xi and Putin on the sidelines of the APEC summit in Shenzhen, and that “among other topics, he will discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons” (BBC). Trump leaves Monday, attends the ASEAN summit in Manila on Tuesday, and meets Xi one-on-one on Thursday before the three-way session (CBS News). A senior official called it “the first conversation” and said no agreement is expected (NBC News). Ukraine is excluded because China “is not really a part of that track”; Iran will be discussed. Officials said export controls will not be discussed (CBS) and that Trump is not expected to raise the Nvidia spy allegations with Xi (ABC). There is no separate Trump–Putin meeting (ABC News).

Why it matters to you. New START, the last U.S.–Russia nuclear treaty, lapsed in February, and Trump turned down Russia’s offer to extend it while he sought a deal that includes China (Al Jazeera). The Pentagon estimates China has more than 600 warheads, against more than 5,000 each for the U.S. and Russia, and Beijing has shown little interest in a pact (NBC News). A meeting that leaves Ukraine to “another track” is also a test of how far Washington will go with Moscow, after last month’s Russian diesel deal and sanctions suspensions worried Europe (BBC). Elon Musk is travelling with Trump. Officials pointed to his business interests in China but did not say why he is going.

Divergence — arms talks, or a stage for something else?

What is in dispute. What the meeting is for. The White House frames it as arms control. The Kremlin’s Dmitry Peskov called it “extremely significant” but rejected comparisons with Yalta, and Zelensky has said he fears it could become a place to discuss a Ukraine deal behind Kyiv’s back (Al Jazeera). Musk’s role is vague: ABC says he joins “parts” of the China travels, and CBS and Al Jazeera say he will not be in the room with Xi and Putin. We could not confirm what Musk will do in China. Why: officials declined to say.

Why the vantage differs. Every U.S. account comes from officials who spoke on condition of anonymity, and they have reason to set expectations low. Moscow gains standing from sitting at a three-power table whatever the outcome. Kyiv has the most to lose from any side conversation it cannot see.

What it means for the reader. Watch for a dated follow-up round on nuclear limits, which would be the first real result, and for whether any readout mentions Ukraine despite the White House saying it is off the table.

Lutnick has agreed to appear before Senate Banking on Thursday, Dec. 10, according to a U.S. official cited by CBS. Axios reported that Trump approved action against chip diverters three times, in May, August and on Nov. 5, and that Bessent confronted Lutnick in the Situation Room (Fox News). NBC calls it long-simmering tension “burst into public view” (NBC News).

There is also a money angle. CNBC found that Cantor Fitzgerald, now run by Lutnick’s sons, cut its reported Nvidia shareholding by nearly half between September 2025 and March 2026, while intelligence officials were pressing Lutnick on Megaspeed. Former Bush White House ethics lawyer Richard Painter said there is “no proof” Lutnick shared nonpublic information but “the appearance doesn’t look great” (CNBC). China’s foreign ministry warned Washington against “abuse of export control measures” (CBS News).

Why it matters to you. When one Cabinet secretary blames another on television, a rule change is usually close behind. The likeliest are a Megaspeed listing or a tighter rule on renting chips through overseas data centers, and either would change where U.S. AI hardware can legally run. Advice to banks and companies on where to build AI capacity will need to account for that. Trump flies to China on Monday with officials saying the chip question is off the table there.

Commerce spokeswoman Kristen Eichamer told CNBC there was “no such conversation.” Bessent: “We don’t repeat conversations in the Situation Room.” We could not confirm the meeting. Why: the sources are unnamed and the participants either deny it or will not discuss it.

Why the vantage differs. Bessent gains by putting distance between Treasury and the scandal. Commerce is defending its secretary. Axios’s sources are unnamed officials, almost certainly from the agencies Bessent names as having favored a listing. The PBS NewsHour segment (PBS) adds a former Biden NSC official, who argues tighter controls would leave China “years behind” rather than months.

What it means for the reader. The Dec. 10 hearing is where this goes on the record. Before then, a Commerce listing of Megaspeed would show who won the internal fight.

The Pan American Health Organization has revoked U.S. measles elimination, the Wall Street Journal reports, as Pennsylvania and Wisconsin bring this year’s deaths to 10

Science & public health Broader domestic institutions Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 (Wall Street Journal, read to the first paragraphs; Al Jazeera, NBC News and ABC News in full). The revocation rests on the Journal’s unnamed sources. Trigger (threshold event): the decision this brief flagged on Nov. 11 as due from a PAHO panel by Friday.

PAHO revoked the status the U.S. has held since 2000, “people familiar with the matter said” (Wall Street Journal). Under PAHO’s rules the test is uninterrupted transmission for more than 12 months; Canada lost its status last year (Al Jazeera). On Friday Wisconsin reported a child’s death and Pennsylvania the death of an unvaccinated Montour County resident: seven deaths in Pennsylvania, two in Wisconsin and one in New York, and none of the three had seen a measles death in more than 30 years (NBC News). At least four of the dead were babies too young to be vaccinated. The CDC counts 5,412 cases in 45 states this year, against an average of 180 a year from 2000 to 2024, and kindergarten coverage has fallen from 95% in 2019 to 92% (ABC News).

Why it matters to you. Losing the status changes no law, but it is the clearest official verdict yet on falling vaccination rates under Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. It also tests whether the federal count can be trusted: Al Jazeera reports that Kennedy asked CDC director Erica Schwartz not to count some deaths, and that the CDC’s own tally shows six. Florida has had no measles deaths in these counts. As this brief noted on Nov. 11, the state is behind on kindergarten vaccination.

Divergence — decided, or due Tuesday? And six deaths or ten?

What is in dispute. Two facts. The Journal reports the revocation as done; NBC, published the same day, says the decision “is expected to be announced on Tuesday,” Nov. 17. We could not confirm the revocation. Why: neither PAHO nor the CDC has announced it, and the CDC’s measles page refused our request. The death count also differs: state health departments report 10 and the CDC lists six.

Why the vantage differs. The Journal’s sources know the panel’s outcome; NBC is reporting the formal announcement date. The difference may only be timing. The death gap is political: states count deaths of patients who tested positive, and Kennedy has disputed that some were caused by measles, which lowers the federal total.

What it means for the reader. Watch Tuesday for a formal PAHO statement and for how the CDC words it. If it plays down the decision or repeats the lower death count, the fight over federal health data will be the next story.

Florida’s Public Counsel tells the state Supreme Court that regulators let FPL and big business write a $7 billion rate deal, with data-center terms that residents say shift costs onto them

Florida / Tampa Bay Data centers / power / AI infrastructure Energy & grid New Single-sourced

Independent accounts compared: 1 newsroom (Tampa Bay Times), the only outlet we found covering Thursday’s briefs. The Florida Supreme Court docket confirms the filings; background from Politico Pro (February) and Food & Water Watch (April). We did not read the briefs, and the residents’ groups’ brief was filed partly as confidential.

Walt Trierweiler, the Legislature’s appointed lawyer for utility customers, filed what the Tampa Bay Times calls a “blistering” brief asking the justices to throw out the Public Service Commission’s approval of FPL’s base-rate increase, which customers have paid since January. The deal was reached between FPL and mostly big-business intervenors, including industrial companies, Walmart and an association of unnamed would-be data-center builders; the Public Counsel did not sign it. “With the help of a few of their fellow corporations, utilities have moved closer to regulating themselves,” Trierweiler wrote, adding that the commission has “become addicted” to settlements. Lawyers for residents’ groups (Florida Rising, LULAC and ECOSWF, per the docket) asked the court to strike the data-center provisions, arguing they make all customers pay for solar plants and other infrastructure mainly serving data centers. The court docket (SC2026-0816) shows the briefs filed Nov. 12 and AARP Florida admitted as a friend of the court. No oral argument has been set.

Why it matters to you. FPL serves about 12 million Floridians, more than half the state. Most of Pasco is served by Duke Energy Florida and the Withlacoochee River co-op rather than FPL, so this is not your bill. The ruling still matters for you, because it decides how much room the Public Service Commission has to approve settlements that skip the Public Counsel, and that commission also sets Duke’s rates. The Times notes that Duke and Tampa Electric filed separate data-center cost plans and FPL did not. In 2024 Carlos Muñiz, then chief justice, called the commission a “black box.”

Divergence — a 2% bill increase, or a rigged process?

What is in dispute. A dispute over interpretation and law. FPL spokesman Andrew Sutton says the deal “amounts to an approximately 2% average annual residential bill increase” and calls its data-center terms “one of the nation’s strongest customer-protection frameworks.” The challengers say it gives FPL an “unconscionable” profit rate and breaks the Legislature’s rule that data centers pay their actual costs.

Why the vantage differs. FPL and the corporate signatories benefit from the deal as approved. The Public Counsel is paid by the state to represent residential customers and lost at the commission. The residents’ groups are represented by Earthjustice lawyers, as Politico Pro reported when the appeal was filed in February. The Times was the only outlet we found that read the new briefs, and it read them through the challengers’ framing plus FPL’s response.

What it means for the reader. The next date is oral argument. A ruling that settlements need the Public Counsel’s agreement, or that data-center costs must be split out, would reach Duke’s customers in Pasco through the next rate case.
