Nvidia’s board met Wednesday night on whether to remove Jensen Huang after a second affair was reported; the scandal is reviving chip-export bills a week before Trump meets Xi

AI & tech industry International with US channel Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 5 newsrooms (CNBC, the Guardian, NBC News, Al Jazeera, and the Washington Post, which we saw only through a search excerpt), plus Reuters via RTÉ. Trigger (new verifiable fact): the board’s meeting on Huang’s future and the New York Times report of a second relationship, both since yesterday’s lead.

Nvidia’s board met late Wednesday to decide whether to remove Huang as chief executive, CNBC confirmed, after the New York Times reported that he had a second extramarital relationship, with Kimberly Powell, the company’s head of health care, who reports to him (CNBC). That came on top of his admission on Sunday to an affair with Alice Huang, a Megaspeed executive whom U.S. intelligence officials believe is a Chinese spy. On Sunday the board said it had confidence in him. Under Nvidia’s bylaws, a majority of the 11 directors can remove him “with or without cause” (Guardian). The White House was told about the meeting, according to NBC News. Bloomberg reported that the board had been working with an outside firm on possible replacements, and a fund manager named CFO Colette Kress as the natural interim pick (Reuters via RTÉ). We could not confirm that the board has reached a decision. Why: as of this morning Nvidia had not commented, and Axios reported no decision as of early Thursday. The Washington Post notes how fast support shifted: on Monday Trump said Huang “had absolutely no idea” the woman was a spy, adding, “I really believe him.”

Why it matters to you. Nvidia’s chips sit under almost every AI build-out, and Huang was, in Eurasia Group’s David Meale’s words, “the industry’s most effective lobbyist against tighter rules.” The scandal has given China hawks of both parties a push to put three chip bills (the AI Overwatch Act, the Chip Security Act and the MATCH Act) into this year’s defense bill, and CNBC reports that Commerce is finishing a rule to stop Chinese firms from renting restricted chips in data centers abroad (CNBC). Trump is expected to meet Xi in Shenzhen on Wednesday and Thursday, Nov. 18–19, at the APEC summit. Democratic Sens. Elizabeth Warren and Chris Coons asked Commerce, State and chief of staff Stephen Miller to preserve all records on Nvidia’s China exports (Guardian). Megaspeed said Thursday it is cooperating with Commerce and has hired a U.S. law firm to count its GPUs; China’s foreign ministry called the spying claims fabricated (NBC). CNBC relays The Information’s report that Alice Huang helped arrange at least $7.5 billion in Nvidia chip shipments to China. We could not confirm that figure. Why: The Information is paywalled and we saw it only as CNBC summarized it.

Divergence — should he stay?

What is in dispute. A dispute over judgment, not facts. Bernstein’s Stacy Rasgon says an affair with a direct report makes it “much tougher for the board to leave him in position,” and Dan Ives says Nvidia “likely now has to move on.” Trump calls Huang “an honorable guy,” and Elon Musk says hiring him would be “an honour.” Seaport’s Jay Goldberg told Al Jazeera that replacing him would be “impossible to do personality-wise.”

Why the vantage differs. Analysts answer to shareholders, who face governance and legal risk from a CEO’s relationship with a subordinate. Trump has relied on Huang in his China dealings and goes to Shenzhen next week. Musk is a rival employer and a Trump ally. Directors have the Wachtell, Lipton investigation in hand, which no outsider has seen.

What it means for the reader. Watch the board’s statement and the defense bill, not the share price. If Huang goes, the loudest voice against export controls goes with him, which makes the chip bills likelier to pass this year.

Dig deeper: the three chip bills

The AI Overwatch Act, the Chip Security Act and the MATCH Act all aim to limit advanced chips reaching U.S. adversaries. CNBC says the MATCH Act would press Japan and the Netherlands to tighten curbs on chipmaking tools already in China, and DGA-Albright Stonebridge’s Paul Triolo warns that Beijing has said it would respond, possibly with rare-earth and magnet controls. Schumer and the House China committee chair, Republican John Moolenaar, both want the bills in the defense bill.

The White House counsel tells the incoming Oversight chair that Democratic probes “may cross the line into blatant criminality,” and a Texas U.S. attorney orders Sen. Blumenthal to preserve his ballroom records

Broader domestic institutions US politics & policy New subject Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 newsrooms (CBS News, NBC News, Al Jazeera, the Guardian), plus Fox News on the new White House structure. We did not read either letter in full.

In a letter dated Monday and released Wednesday, White House counsel Will Scharf told Rep. Robert Garcia, who is set to chair the House Oversight Committee from January, that threats against officials or subpoenas meant “to abuse or instill fear in the recipient may cross the line into blatant criminality,” and that “issuing unlawful subpoenas” may “warrant further investigation” (CBS News). Garcia answered: “It appears Donald Trump has something to hide.” The letter does not say what the White House would do (Al Jazeera).

The first step went further. Aaron Reitz, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas and a former chief of staff to Sen. Ted Cruz, sent Sen. Richard Blumenthal a records-preservation demand over Blumenthal’s inquiry into anonymous donors to the roughly $400 million White House ballroom, citing the federal conspiracy-against-rights and color-of-law statutes. “I’m taking the first step to stop this abuse of power,” Reitz posted on X. The acting deputy attorney general, Trent McCotter, backed him. NBC reports that a step like this against a sitting member would typically go through DOJ’s Public Integrity office, which has been shrunk and sidelined. Blumenthal said he “will not be intimidated” and sent Reitz a preservation demand of his own (NBC News; NBC News). This comes in Stephen Miller’s first week as chief of staff. A memo from his office gives Cabinet secretaries more control of their own messaging, according to Fox News. Why it matters to you: whether Congress can subpoena the executive branch in 2027 will decide how much you learn about tariffs, the war, the ballroom donors and the pardons Democrats say they will investigate.

Divergence — oversight or abuse?

What is in dispute. A dispute over law. Scharf says congressional inquiries need a “legitimate legislative purpose” and that Democrats want to “exercise powers of criminal prosecution.” Garcia says Congress’s authority “comes from Article I of the Constitution, not from the permission of the White House.” DOJ says Blumenthal’s letters to donors threaten their First Amendment rights.

Why the vantage differs. The White House has been hiring lawyers for its counsel’s office ahead of the January subpoenas, NBC reports. Garcia and Blumenthal are building the investigations Democrats ran on. Reitz is a political appointee who ran for Texas attorney general. Each side’s account is a legal position, not a court ruling.

What it means for the reader. Expect the first fights in court, not on the House floor. Neither letter is a court ruling, and no charge has been filed against anyone.

Colom wins Mississippi and Hyde-Smith concedes; Democrats will hold at least 55 Senate seats, and Republicans are rushing judges through before January

Elections and electoral process US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 newsrooms (NBC News, CNBC, the Guardian, CBS News). Trigger (threshold event): the race was called and conceded Wednesday. Yesterday it sat in Also noted as uncalled.

Democrat Scott Colom, a district attorney, beat Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith, according to the AP call cited by CNBC. Hyde-Smith called to concede: “I respect the decision of Mississippi voters.” Mississippi last elected a Democratic senator 44 years ago, and Colom will be its first Black senator since Reconstruction. Trump carried the state by 23 points in 2024. NBC’s exit poll found majorities of Mississippi voters disapproved of Trump, said tariffs hurt the economy and said military action in Iran was wrong (NBC News). Colom’s pitch: “We were never a lost cause. We were a betrayed cause,” blaming Hyde-Smith over tariffs, health coverage and the war, and his own party for not competing. The Guardian reports he won with little help from national Democrats. It is the eighth Senate seat Democrats have flipped. Alaska is the only race still uncalled, with Democrat Mary Peltola narrowly ahead of Sen. Dan Sullivan.

What changes in January. The Senate confirms judges, Cabinet officers and Fed nominees. Trump has had 57 judges confirmed this term. There are 28 vacancies and 15 more coming, and he has sent 16 nominees. The Republican-run Judiciary Committee advanced four district-court picks and held hearings on four more on Tuesday, and plans more hearings next week, breaking its usual every-other-week schedule (CBS News). Sen. Dick Durbin says Republicans are trying to seat “more than 30 qualified loyalists” before the handover, and objects to two Texas picks: T. Elliot Gaiser, who runs DOJ’s Office of Legal Counsel, and James Sullivan, who just lost re-election to the Texas Supreme Court. “No one, or nearly no one, is going to get confirmed” after January, said the conservative legal scholar Ed Whelan. Why it matters to you: a 55-seat Democratic Senate can block nominees, though it is well short of the 67 votes needed to override a veto.

The talks. Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner landed in Moscow on Thursday after two days of talks in Abu Dhabi, the first in nine months, which produced a large prisoner exchange and no breakthrough. “There’s no trust right now between the sides,” Kushner said. He wants European countries added to the format, which Moscow has resisted, and the envoys then fly to Dublin to see Zelenskyy, who says Ukraine won’t cede occupied territory (NBC News). Why it matters to you: a satellite network that the BBC describes as SpaceX-owned, and that Musk says the U.S. government controls, is now shaping the battlefield during U.S.-brokered talks, and nobody has said on the record who sets its limits.

Divergence — who turned the dial?

What is in dispute. A dispute over fact. Zelenskyy says the limits are deliberate. In October, Musk first wrote that “Starshield systems are owned and controlled by the US government,” then said Ukraine broke Starlink’s terms. The Wall Street Journal reported Musk ordered the October limits and that Trump asked him to reverse them. Trump denies the White House ordered anything.

Why the vantage differs. Kyiv wants U.S. pressure on SpaceX and has an interest in showing harm. Musk wants to avoid blame from both Washington and Kyiv. The White House is mediating and doesn’t want to be seen squeezing Ukraine during talks. Only SpaceX’s logs or a government contract officer could settle it, and neither has spoken.

What it means for the reader. If the limits line up with the Moscow meeting, that looks like leverage; if SpaceX restores speeds within days, as in October, it may be a contract dispute. Either way, a single company controls a battlefield lever.

ICE reports a record 483,200 deportations for fiscal 2026 and is aiming for about 19,000 arrests a week under Stephen Miller

Broader domestic institutions Florida / Tampa Bay New subject Single-sourced

Independent accounts compared: 1 (CBS News, which obtained the DHS figures; we found no other newsroom with them this morning), plus DHS’s own Nov. 3 release for context. Underreported: the figures came out at 6 a.m. and had not reached other fronts we swept.

ICE deported 483,200 people in the fiscal year that ended Sept. 30, up from its previous record of 442,640 in fiscal 2025, and made more than 491,000 immigration arrests, nearly 475,000 of them administrative arrests for civil violations, according to DHS figures obtained by CBS News. DHS says the total is about 18% above the Obama-era benchmark of 409,849 in fiscal 2012 and nearly 78% above fiscal 2024. ICE made 50,925 arrests in August alone. “I couldn’t be prouder,” said DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin. CBS’s sources say ICE has been told to aim for about 19,000 arrests a week, a target set before the midterms, and officials don’t expect the pace to slow now that Miller, who pushed for higher targets, is chief of staff. The 2025 tax-and-spending law gave ICE about $75 billion, including $45 billion for detention, money a Democratic Congress can’t easily claw back. DHS’s Nov. 3 release separately claims nearly 1.2 million deportations since Trump returned to office.

Why it matters to you. Trump has said parts of the campaign may have gone too far, and CBS polling shows discomfort with how enforcement is carried out, yet the arrest target and the money are already in place. That leaves the January Congress with oversight more than funding as its lever. We found no Florida or Tampa Bay breakdown of the figures.

Divergence — what counts as a deportation?

What is in dispute. A dispute over numbers. DHS calls 483,200 the highest in ICE’s history. Syracuse University’s TRAC, using case-by-case court records, found formal removals were only 63% of deportations in July and August 2026, with the rest voluntary departures and returns, as CBS reports. CBS also notes that by broader governmentwide measures, deportations were higher in parts of the early 2000s.

Why the vantage differs. DHS is making a political case after an election in which enforcement was an issue, and it chooses what to count. TRAC is an independent research group that works from immigration-court records. CBS saw the DHS data, not the underlying case files.

What it means for the reader. The record is real by ICE’s own count, but much of it may be people pressured to leave rather than formally removed. That difference matters for court challenges and for what a Democratic Congress can investigate.

The American Cancer Society now recommends a PSA test every other year from age 50, reversing years of caution

Science & public healthSingle-sourced

Independent accounts compared: 1 (NBC News). We did not read the guideline in CA: A Cancer Journal for Clinicians.

The new guidelines, published Thursday, advise men at average risk to get the prostate-specific antigen blood test every two years after 50, annual testing from 45 for two higher-risk groups, and a conversation with a doctor at 70 to 74. Previously the Cancer Society advised “shared decision-making” (NBC News). The change rests largely on a European randomized trial with more than two decades of follow-up showing screening lowers prostate cancer deaths. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommended against PSA testing at any age in 2012, and late-stage diagnoses then crept up. Why it matters: it changes the default advice for most men over 50, and the old worry, overtreatment, has not gone away.
