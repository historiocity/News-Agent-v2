Iran hits about 25 tankers in a week and strikes at U.S. bases in Bahrain; Trump says he wants a “permanent” deal; and Democrats split over risking a Dec. 11 shutdown to cut off war money

Geopolitics Core — US politics & policy Continuing Independent corroboration

Continuing lead (Iran’s widening strikes and the Pentagon money fight ran Nov. 19). Trigger: threshold event — the most tanker strikes in a single week that we have seen reported, and attacks on Bahrain after a lull of about two months, by CBS News’s count; and a material connection — the war is now the dividing line in the Dec. 11 funding deadline and in the Dec. 1 vote for Senate Democratic leader.

Independent accounts compared: 6 newsrooms (Al Jazeera, CBS News, CNBC carrying Reuters, The Hill, Axios, Common Dreams), plus the Guardian on Vance.

Al Jazeera’s Tehran correspondent counts about 25 tankers hit in and around the Strait of Hormuz since Nov. 14. Britain’s UKMTO counted 263 ships through the strait from Nov. 12 to 18, 15% fewer than the week before, and 37 oil tankers, down from 52. The Revolutionary Guard says it hit U.S. positions at the al-Riffa and Awali bases in Bahrain and destroyed two Patriot radars, which we could not confirm; the claim comes only from the IRGC. U.S. Central Command denied an Iranian claim that a U.S. F-16 was damaged (Al Jazeera). Bahrain said a strike on Saturday hit a civilian industrial facility, its fourth attempted attack in about a week, and on Saturday UKMTO reported two tankers hit by unidentified projectiles near Oman’s Khasab (CBS News; CNBC / Reuters). Trump told Fox News on Saturday he wants a “permanent” deal so that a future “incompetent president” could not let Iran get a nuclear weapon. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi said the talks must be “given a chance” and the U.S. blockade “must be lifted rationally.”

In Washington, the money is the fight. Funding for most of the government runs out on Friday, Dec. 11. Rep. Ro Khanna calls cutting war money a red line: “I do think we’ve got to get that money out.” Rep. Rosa DeLauro, the top Democrat on House Appropriations, says it is “not a red line” and that “shutting the government down doesn’t stop the war”; Rep. Gregory Meeks talks of conditions and amendments instead (The Hill, read through a search excerpt). Axios reports that Republican leaders will probably need a two-thirds vote, and so many Democratic votes, to pass any stopgap (Axios, read through a search excerpt). In the Senate, Chris Van Hollen is challenging Chuck Schumer for leader on a demand to halt war funding now; the secret-ballot vote is set for Tuesday, Dec. 1, and he needs 28 votes (Common Dreams). At the White House on Friday, Vance stopped short of endorsing Trump’s claim that Iran was weeks from a bomb in February (The Guardian).

Why it matters to you. The war is now driving three things at once: oil and shipping costs that reach fuel prices in Florida, the first big test of the new Democratic majority before it is even seated, and who leads the Senate. A shutdown fight over war money would land in the middle of the holidays.

Divergence — how to end a war Democrats all oppose

Dispute type. Strategy, not goal. Khanna and Rep. Yassamin Ansari will oppose any bill that does not bar money for offensive operations; DeLauro, Meeks and centrists such as Rep. Brad Schneider want to avoid a shutdown and use the January majority.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. DeLauro negotiates the spending bill and wants the January agenda on schedule. Khanna and Van Hollen speak to an activist base energized by the midterms, and Van Hollen’s leadership bid turns on the issue. The Hill and Axios quote members directly; Common Dreams is an advocacy outlet friendly to the Van Hollen camp. Iran’s battle claims come from state media and are often denied by CENTCOM.

What it means for you. A short stopgap with no war limits is the likeliest path, by the members’ own accounts, but it depends on how many Democrats follow Khanna. Watch the Dec. 1 Senate vote as the first count.

Trump says he would work with the incoming Democratic Congress to cap credit-card interest, reviving a 10% cap the banks beat back in January

Core — US financials Core — US politics & policy New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 2 newsrooms (the Associated Press, carried by PBS and CBS; Axios), plus statements from the bank trade groups and the American Enterprise Institute.

“I would do it. These credit card companies have made a lot,” Trump told Fox News on Saturday. Sen. Elizabeth Warren, expected to chair Senate Banking in January, answered: “Let’s cap credit card interest rates and save Americans money.” The usual proposal is 10%; the median card rate is about 22.2%, according to a New York Fed survey in August. Researchers cited by AP estimate a 10% cap would save Americans about $100 billion a year in interest while leaving the industry profitable with fewer rewards. Rep. Maxine Waters and, on the Republican side, Sen. Josh Hawley have backed caps (PBS / AP). CBS works the arithmetic: on a $5,000 balance, interest would fall from about $100 a month to about $42 (CBS News). Trump also attacked fees: “a bill that some guy set up in an office on Park Avenue” (Axios, read through a search excerpt).

Trump promised a one-year 10% cap in January; nothing came of it after the industry pushed back and the Republican Congress showed little interest. Five bank groups, including the American Bankers Association and the Bank Policy Institute, said then that a cap “would reduce credit availability” and push borrowers to “less regulated, more costly alternatives” (ABA). On Nov. 17 AEI’s Paul Kupiec argued that a cap would mean cancelled near-prime accounts, higher fees and rationed rewards (AEI). There is no bill text or White House draft yet; we could not find one.

Why it matters to you. JPMorgan and its direct competitors are among the big card issuers, and their trade groups led the January pushback. A president and a Democratic Banking chair on the same side is the first real path a cap has had, and it would reshape rewards, fees and who gets a card.

Divergence — relief or rationing?

Dispute type. Forecast. Supporters cite about $100 billion a year in savings; the banks and AEI forecast closed accounts for weaker borrowers and higher fees for everyone else. An ABA survey by Morning Consult found 55% to 30% opposition to a 10% cap if it meant higher annual fees.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. Trump and Warren have both made affordability their pitch to voters. The banks would lose interest income, and the ABA commissioned the survey, whose questions were framed around costs to cardholders. AEI is a free-market think tank. The savings figure comes from researchers AP did not name in the copy we read.

What it means for you. Both sides agree a cap would cut card profits; they disagree on who pays. The first test is whether Warren schedules a markup in January.

Federal emergency orders keeping an Orlando coal unit open have cost ratepayers $25.6 million, an analysis finds; Washington cites Florida’s data-center demand, and the latest order expires Nov. 30

Energy & grid Florida / Tampa Bay Data centers / power / AI infrastructure New Primary source

Independent accounts compared: 2 newsrooms (Inside Climate News, published by WUSF; Central Florida Public Media), plus the Energy Department’s order itself.

The Orlando Utilities Commission planned to put Stanton Unit 1, a 464.5-megawatt coal unit running since 1987, into “extended cold shutdown” in mid-2026. The Energy Department ordered it kept available under the emergency power in section 202(c) of the Federal Power Act, first on June 4 and again on Sept. 1, in Order No. 202-26-42, which runs through Monday, Nov. 30 (Energy Department). The order finds an emergency in the “Florida Peninsula” grid region while noting that the national reliability organization, NERC, rates the area at “Normal Risk” for long-term adequacy; it rests the case on forecasts in which new generation and transmission lag behind “new data centers and other large loads.”

An Environmental Defense Fund analysis puts Stanton’s cost to ratepayers at $25.6 million, about $6.2 million a month, and $593 million for six such plants nationwide. A DOE spokesperson called the critics “radical climate activists” and said the orders “likely saved hundreds of American lives” at peak demand. OUC, which serves more than 288,000 customers in Orange and Osceola counties, says the cost claims rest on assumptions that “do not reflect” its own analysis, and its board will weigh a fuel-rate cut of about $5 a month in December. In Michigan, DOE issued a seventh order for the Campbell plant on Nov. 13, after the D.C. Circuit found in September that there was no emergency there (WUSF / Inside Climate News). Health experts and local leaders told Central Florida Public Media last week the unit poses significant risks to public health (Central Florida Public Media). We could not read EDF’s analysis itself; the figures are as WUSF reported them.

Why it matters to you. Data-center demand forecasts are part of Washington’s case for keeping an old Florida plant running, and someone has now put a price on it. The bill lands on Orlando-area customers, not Pasco’s, but the same regional demand argument could be made about Tampa Bay’s utilities.

Divergence — emergency or expensive insurance?

Dispute type. Factual and legal: whether an emergency exists, and what the unit costs to run. DOE says it does and the unit saves lives; EDF, Earthjustice and the Sierra Club say there is no emergency and the cost is $25.6 million; OUC disputes the cost math but complies.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. DOE wrote the order and defends its own authority. EDF and Earthjustice are suing over it, so their estimate is advocacy as well as analysis. OUC has the actual fuel and operating data and a board vote coming in December. The D.C. Circuit’s Michigan ruling is the only court finding so far.

What it means for you. Expect a third order before Nov. 30 if DOE follows its Michigan pattern. Florida’s first court test of the orders would decide whether the data-center argument holds up.

New York asks a state judge on Monday to stop Kalshi’s sports contracts after a federal judge sent the case back and another refused the CFTC’s bid to block the hearing

AI and financial services Broader domestic institutions New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 2 newsrooms (ABC News, CNBC). CNBC discloses a commercial relationship with Kalshi and a minority investment in it.

On Friday, U.S. District Judge Vernon Broderick refused to move New York Attorney General Letitia James’s suit against Kalshi into federal court, ruling that prediction markets do not necessarily fall outside state jurisdiction. James then asked state Judge Melissa Crane for a temporary restraining order to stop Kalshi offering sports contracts without a state gaming license; a hearing is set for Monday. Kalshi’s lawyer said the order would “likely force the liquidation of all open positions.” In an emergency call, a lawyer for the Commodity Futures Trading Commission asked a second federal judge, Lorna Schofield, to stop Crane from ruling, saying the order “would completely shut down Kalshi’s operations nationwide.” Schofield refused: “The requested application is denied” (ABC News).

It is one front in a wider war. Utah has filed 14 criminal charges against Kalshi and its founders, who are due in court on Dec. 4; a Louisiana judge granted a restraining order on Nov. 12; the CFTC has sued 12 states; and the Fourth Circuit ruled 2–1 this week that Maryland can enforce its gambling law. The Senate on Thursday passed a bipartisan bill barring contracts on terrorism, assassination or war. Kalshi ended its advisory deal with Donald Trump Jr. on Nov. 12, pointing to his firm 1789 Capital’s stake in rival Polymarket (CNBC).

Why it matters to you. You flagged prediction-market regulation as a watch item to promote on an enforcement action; a state court order that could close out positions nationwide is that. The fight also sets whether event contracts are regulated as finance or as gambling, which matters for every bank and broker building on them.

Divergence — market disruption or a licensing rule?

Dispute type. Legal and factual. The CFTC and Kalshi say a state order would shut the platform nationwide; New York’s lawyer Kate Rhodes Janofsky said “there has been no evidence of market disruption” and the suit does not seek to close Kalshi.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. The CFTC is defending its claim to exclusive jurisdiction and has a single sitting commissioner. Kalshi is a $40 billion company, by Rep. Ro Khanna’s count, with every incentive to warn of chaos. James is enforcing state gambling law. ABC had the hearing record; CNBC has a commercial tie to Kalshi, which it discloses.

What it means for you. Courts are splitting, and most observers expect the Supreme Court to settle it. Monday’s ruling is the near-term marker.

Republican senators turn on Howard Lutnick: Tom Cotton says Commerce ignored his “many letters” about the Megaspeed chip pipeline, and Jensen Huang agrees to testify in public

Core — AI & tech industry Core — US politics & policy Continuing Primary source

Continuing lead (Lutnick, Cantor and the chip licenses ran Nov. 18 and 20). Trigger: new verifiable fact — the Republican Senate intelligence chair now says on the record that Commerce ignored his warnings, and his October 2025 letter asking Commerce to investigate Megaspeed is public; and a dated event — Huang’s House interview on Dec. 1 and his pledge to testify publicly in the Senate.

Independent accounts compared: 2 newsrooms (The Guardian, CNBC), plus Sen. Cotton’s own 2025 letter and The Hill’s headline on his remarks.

Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia’s chief executive this month over extramarital affairs, including one with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed whom U.S. intelligence agencies suspect of being a Chinese spy. Megaspeed allegedly served as a conduit for $4 billion of Nvidia chips diverted to China. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has said Treasury, Defense, State and the trade representative wanted Megaspeed blacklisted months ago and that “Commerce has the pen.” Cotton told the Wall Street Journal Lutnick should have taken “the threats of communist China more seriously”; Sen. John Kennedy said Commerce “greenlit $4bn of sales” to “the company of the spy” and wants to know why. Ron Wyden and Richard Neal have told Lutnick to preserve all records on Nvidia export decisions. Trump gave Lutnick a vote of confidence on Friday (The Guardian; The Hill, headline only).

The paper trail is real: on Oct. 14, 2025, Cotton released a letter asking Lutnick to investigate Megaspeed, a spin-off of a Chinese state-owned cloud company, for “suspicious ownership, affiliations, or ties to U.S chip companies” (Sen. Tom Cotton). Huang has agreed to a closed-door, transcribed interview with three House committees on Tuesday, Dec. 1, and will testify publicly before Senate Banking; Chairman Tim Scott asked for Dec. 3 and wants Lutnick too “before Christmas,” but no date is set (CNBC). We could not find a Commerce Department response to Cotton’s account in the reports we read, and we could not confirm the $4 billion figure beyond the Guardian’s reporting of the allegation.

Why it matters to you. Export-control enforcement decides which AI chips reach China and which data centers get built where. If the story moves from Huang’s private life to what Commerce knew, it becomes a fight over the department that licenses the AI supply chain.

Divergence — who sat on the warnings?

Dispute type. Factual, inside the administration. Bessent places the delay at Commerce; Trump backs Lutnick; Cotton and Kennedy, both Republicans, say Commerce ignored warnings.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. Bessent is shifting blame away from Treasury in a public dispute. Cotton chairs Senate Intelligence and wrote some of the warnings himself, so he has a record to defend as well as a China position to press. The Guardian piece leans on a Democratic adviser for framing. None of the accounts includes Commerce’s internal record, which Wyden and Neal want preserved for the new Congress.

What it means for you. Sworn testimony in December is the first chance to test any of these accounts. Until then, the confirmed fact is Cotton’s 2025 letter, not what Commerce did with it.
