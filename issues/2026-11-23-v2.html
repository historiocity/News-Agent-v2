Officials tell Americans to expect more “glitches” as Delta’s systems fail at the start of Thanksgiving week, and the spy chief now doubles as acting cyber director

Core — AI & tech industry Broader domestic institutions Continuing Independent corroboration

Continuing lead (the outages ran Nov. 18 and the cyber director’s exit Nov. 20). Trigger: new verifiable fact — Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton will also serve as acting national cyber director, which he confirmed in a statement; and a threshold event — a Delta systems failure cancelled 202 flights on Sunday as about 6 million people prepare to fly this week.

Independent accounts compared: 7 newsrooms (Axios, CNBC, CBS News, NBC News, The Independent, the Daily Wire, The Guardian), plus the White House’s own announcement.

Clayton will run the Office of the National Cyber Director until the Senate confirms Kirsten Davies, the Pentagon’s chief information officer, whom Trump named on Nov. 19 to replace Sean Cairncross (Axios, read through a search excerpt; White House). Clayton is already the administration’s AI czar, so one official now explains the outages, assesses foreign threats and sets cyber defense policy. On CNN on Sunday he said Americans should be prepared for “bumps in the road” as old systems are rebuilt, and that “we are going to have glitches, gaps, interruptions in service” (CNBC; The Independent). Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CBS that bank customers should not worry, and that patches banks are making against AI-driven attacks “may cause a glitch, which is what happened last week. J.P. Morgan went in”; he compared it to a vaccine that makes “your arm” hurt (CBS News transcript). Chase’s Nov. 16 outage, which the bank blamed on a software bug, left some customers unable to reach their accounts or withdraw funds (NBC News).

Travel is where it bites this week. On Sunday a network failure in Delta’s baggage and weight-and-balance system forced a ground delay at Atlanta; Delta cancelled 202 flights, about 4% of the day, and said about 70% of more than 1,900 delays were tied to the automation. United had a similar failure a week earlier, and AAA expects nearly 6 million domestic flyers for the holiday (CBS News). The FAA’s nationwide ground stop of nearly five hours on Nov. 13 was blamed on a router upgrade. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and FAA chief Bryan Bedford have called claims that AI played a role “completely unfounded,” after Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said on Fox that an unnamed tech expert blamed self-fixing AI code (Daily Wire). Readers who wrote to the Guardian blamed staff cuts and asked why one contractor’s router could stop every flight (The Guardian). The Tampa Bay Times reports that Tampa-area airports are preparing for the rush; we could read only its headline (Tampa Bay Times). We could not confirm that Clayton’s acting role has a set end date; Axios says it lasts until Davies is confirmed, and no hearing is scheduled.

Why it matters to you. If you fly from Tampa this week or bank online, the people in charge are telling you to expect more breakdowns, not fewer. Their stated cause, rushed upgrades against AI-era threats, also means more outages while the patching goes on.

Divergence — what is causing the outages, and who is in charge

Dispute type. Facts and significance. Clayton, Duffy, the FAA and Microsoft say the failures were separate upgrade mistakes with no sign of an attack. Bessent ties the bank glitches to AI-driven security patches. Mullin floated AI-written code as a cause. Members of the public, in the Guardian’s reader responses, doubt the official story.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. Officials have intelligence access but also a stake in calming the public before the holiday; Clayton is now grading his own response. Mullin cited one unnamed expert. The Daily Wire, a conservative outlet, and former Trump aides it quotes want one White House message; the Guardian piece is self-selected reader testimony, not a survey.

What it means for you. No official account yet names a single cause for all the failures. Plan travel and payments with a backup (a paper boarding pass, some cash) and treat claims of an attack, or of no risk at all, with care until a written review is published.

A 9–8 appeals court says the House broke the Constitution by counting proxies to pass the 2022 spending law; it blocks the pregnant-workers law only in Texas, but the reasoning reaches the rest of the bill

Core — US politics & policy Broader domestic institutions New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 newsrooms (AP via Fox 13, Bloomberg Law, CourtNews, the Washington Post), plus the opinion as posted on Justia.

The full Fifth Circuit ruled on Friday, Nov. 20, in Texas v. Blanche that the House could not count members “present” by proxy toward a quorum. When the House passed the $1.7 trillion Consolidated Appropriations Act on Dec. 23, 2022, by 225–201, the court counted 226 members recorded by proxy, so fewer than a majority were in the room (CourtNews; opinion on Justia, read through a search excerpt). “‘Proxy presence’ does not count,” Judge Cory Wilson wrote. The court kept in place an order that bars federal enforcement of the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, which requires employers to accommodate pregnancy, against Texas and its agencies, and nothing broader (Bloomberg Law). Advocates say the ruling invalidates the law in Texas, Louisiana and Mississippi; A Better Balance expects an appeal to the Supreme Court. The Trump Justice Department has defended the law (Fox 13 / AP).

The same bill carried far more than the pregnancy law, including the SECURE 2.0 retirement changes and the Electoral Count Reform Act (congress.gov, H.R. 2617). Election-law scholar Rick Hasen has pointed to the electoral-count law as exposed; a brief filed for Sen. Mitch McConnell warned that a strict presence rule could threaten Senate habits such as voice votes (CourtNews). Bloomberg Law notes that a Democrat won the Texas attorney general race, so the office that brought the case changes hands in January. The government has 90 days to seek Supreme Court review, which puts the deadline around Feb. 18. We could not confirm whether the Justice Department will ask; it has not said.

Why it matters to you. Florida sits in the Eleventh Circuit, so the pregnancy law still applies to Florida employers. The bigger stake is legal certainty: retirement-plan rules that employers and advisers already rely on, and the 2022 law that governs how Congress counts electoral votes in 2029, came out of the same vote.

Divergence — a narrow ruling or a broad one

Dispute type. Significance. The majority says its decision covers only Texas’s challenge to one law. Judge James Graves, for seven dissenters, asked why, if the whole act was passed unconstitutionally, the judgment covers “one small sliver” of it.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. Texas’s outgoing Republican attorney general brought the case; the federal government, under both parties, defended the law; A Better Balance wrote the law and is an advocate. Bloomberg Law and CourtNews read the full opinion; the Post and AP report it for general readers. The ruling lands six weeks before Texas’s new Democratic attorney general takes office.

What it means for you. Nothing changes in Florida today. Watch whether the Justice Department appeals and whether anyone sues over another part of the 2022 bill; either would turn a Texas case into a national one.

The Labor Department publishes its rule making it easier to treat workers as independent contractors; it takes effect Jan. 22 and also governs family leave

AI and financial services Core — US politics & policy New Primary source

Independent accounts compared: 2 newsrooms (Transport Topics, Reuters via a search excerpt), plus the Federal Register text, the Labor Department’s page (search excerpt) and law firm Littler.

The final rule, published today, replaces the 2024 six-factor test for who counts as an employee under federal wage law with a test much like the 2021 one, built on two “core” factors: how much control a worker has over the work, and the worker’s chance for profit or loss from initiative or investment. It applies the same test to the Family and Medical Leave Act and takes effect Jan. 22, 2027 (Federal Register). The department says it drew more than 16,000 comments, added examples for digital-platform workers, truck drivers and farm labor contractors, and that the rule does not change classification for tax purposes or override state law (Labor Department, read through a search excerpt). The American Trucking Associations says it protects 350,000 independent truckers (Transport Topics). Reuters framed it as making it easier for companies to classify workers as contractors, who are not owed minimum wage, overtime or unemployment insurance (Reuters, search excerpt only). Littler, a management-side firm, notes there is still no bright-line test and a contractor agreement alone does not decide status (Littler). We could not read the full 97-page preamble; the summary above comes from its abstract and the department’s page.

Why it matters to you. If you work as, or hire, consultants on 1099 terms, this is the federal test that decides whether overtime and family leave apply. The incoming Democratic Congress cannot easily undo it: a disapproval resolution would need Trump’s signature.

Divergence — clarity or lost protections

Dispute type. Significance. The department and trucking groups call it clarity for people who choose to work for themselves; Reuters’s framing, and worker advocates in past rounds, stress that contractors lose wage-law protections.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. The department wrote the rule; ATA lobbied for it; Littler advises employers. Reuters reported the announcement on Nov. 19; today’s publication starts the 60-day clock. We found no worker-group statement on today’s text.

What it means for you. Contracts and worker practices set before Jan. 22 will be judged by this test. The rule has swung with every administration since 2021, so expect a lawsuit and, in 2029, possibly another rewrite.

The Army says it will detonate five tons of degraded explosives at an Iowa plant on Wednesday, arguing it does not have to obey a court order pausing it

Broader domestic institutions New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 2 newsrooms (CBS News, BBC), plus AP and The Hill headlines.

The Army found in March that 68 drums of lead azide, a volatile detonator compound stored for decades at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, had begun to recrystallize. After environmental groups sued, a federal court paused the planned blast on Friday; CBS reports that the Eighth Circuit granted the emergency motion. In a filing late Saturday, the Justice Department said the Army will rely on an emergency exclusion in its EPA permit and act anyway, because falling temperatures raise the risk of an uncontrolled explosion: “Protection of human health and the environment demand action now, not delay” (BBC). On Sunday night the Army said it would detonate on Wednesday. The Iowa Environmental Council, Sierra Club and Earthjustice filed expert declarations disputing the risk of waiting and said “this emergency is of the Army’s own making”; Iowa’s Democratic senator-elect Josh Turek called for a full investigation (CBS News). Residents near the plant had earlier been told to stay indoors for six hours because of a possible lead-dust plume reaching into Illinois (BBC). We could not confirm the court’s response to the Army’s filing; none had been reported by press time.

Why it matters to you. The safety call may be right. The precedent is the issue: a federal agency telling a court it will act despite a pause order, under an emergency clause it reads for itself, the same week the White House limited what contractors may tell Congress.

Divergence — Tuesday or Wednesday, and whose emergency

Dispute type. Facts. The BBC, citing Saturday’s filing, says the Army would act by Nov. 24 because cold made delay dangerous; CBS, reporting the Army’s Sunday-night post, says Wednesday for “optimal weather conditions,” and notes the Army did not explain why a later date became safer.

Vantage, interests, access, timing. The Army and EPA have the technical data but also the liability for decades of storage. The plaintiffs are advocacy groups with their own experts. CBS’s account is the later one.

What it means for you. The shifting date weakens the Army’s claim that it cannot wait for a judge. Watch whether the court rules before Wednesday, and whether the Army proceeds if it does not.
