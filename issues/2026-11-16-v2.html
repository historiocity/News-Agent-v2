Records show the FAA extended L3Harris’s telecom contract ten times without proper authorization as the company spent $23.9 million lobbying; Duffy says he is “stuck with” it and wants more money

Broader domestic institutions US politics & policy Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 5 newsrooms (CBS News, NBC News, Fox News, BBC, Al Jazeera), plus Wall Street Journal and Fox Business headlines as signal only. The contract and lobbying record comes from CBS alone.

Continuation trigger: new verifiable fact. CBS’s review of inspector-general audits and Senate lobbying filings ties Friday’s failure to a contract the FAA kept extending without competition, and Duffy has now named the two routers under test.

CBS reports that a 2024 audit by the Transportation Department’s inspector general found the FAA made 10 “improperly authorized” extensions to the telecom contract it first awarded to Harris Corporation, now L3Harris, in 2002, adding 57 months and $1.5 billion. The audit warned the FAA “may be repetitively using these noncompetitive extensions for convenience.” L3Harris has paid $23.9 million to lobbyists since 2020, including Ballard Partners, a firm with deep White House ties. Its PAC gave to Transportation Committee chairman Sam Graves and ranking Democrat Rick Larsen. Congress later put $12.5 billion for FAA modernization, including $4.75 billion for telecom, into the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (CBS News). Duffy told Fox News that L3Harris has taken routers from sites in Texas and Arkansas to its Florida headquarters for “deep diagnostics,” with FAA, Juniper and Cisco engineers, and that there is no sign of a foreign cyberattack (Fox News). NBC says a copper-to-fiber swap in El Dorado, Arkansas, set off a recently replaced router in Amarillo, Texas, which flooded other centers. More than 3,500 of 5,170 lines have been converted, with the rest due by 2028 (NBC News).

Why it matters to you. Whoever gets the next tranche of air-traffic money, and on what terms, decides whether this happens again before Thanksgiving travel. The work is being tested at L3Harris’s Florida headquarters, and the Wall Street Journal calls the company Washington’s go-to fixer for troubled projects (WSJ, headline only). Duffy refused to apologize on “Meet the Press,” blaming decades of neglect. Trump wants another $50 billion, while Democrats point to money already allocated and unspent (Al Jazeera). By 7 a.m. Monday, FlightAware counted about 8,365 delays and 533 cancellations, down from 26,392 and 2,858 on Sunday (NBC News). We could not confirm whether the fault was the installation or the equipment. Why: the FAA says it still does not know, and L3Harris’s tests are not finished.

Divergence — who owns the failure, and how big was it?

What is in dispute. A dispute over interpretation and over numbers. Duffy says “it’s all of them,” meaning past administrations of both parties. He also says contractors “take our money and they provide really poor service.” Democrats say the problem is execution, not funding. The scale differs too: Al Jazeera reports more than 9,000 cancellations and about 1.5 million stranded passengers; the BBC, citing Bloomberg, says cancellations passed 17,000 by Sunday; Fox counts about 15,000 flights disrupted on Friday and Saturday.

Why the vantage differs. Duffy is asking Congress for money and needs the failure to look inherited. L3Harris is defending a contract it has held for 24 years and says 70% of the copper-to-fiber switch is done (CBS). Democrats take the gavels in January and gain from showing waste. The tallies differ because they count different days and either cancellations alone or delays and cancellations together.

What it means for the reader. Use FlightAware’s daily counts for the size of the disruption, not the summary numbers. The policy test is whether new money comes with competition for the work or simply extends the same contract.

Dig deeper: the audit trail CBS assembled

2006: an audit warns the program is behind schedule. 2008: a follow-up flags reliability problems. 2009: a telecom outage delays more than 800 flights, and the inspector general finds the FAA failed to oversee Harris. 2012: Harris wins a $291 million voice-equipment contract that is cancelled in 2018 after $160 million is spent. 2024: the audit of the 10 improper extensions. September 2026: the inspector general warns that rushing upgrades could itself cause disruptions. All per CBS News; we did not read the audit PDFs ourselves.

China confirms Xi will host Trump and Putin in Shenzhen on Thursday; Cantwell accuses Lutnick’s Commerce Department of letting top AI chips reach China, and a Warren–Coons document deadline falls Tuesday

International with US channel Core — AI & tech industry Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4 newsrooms (CNBC, NPR, NBC News, Semafor), plus a Wall Street Journal headline as signal.

Continuation trigger: threshold event and new verifiable fact. Beijing’s Monday confirmation fixes the date, and Sen. Maria Cantwell’s Sunday letter puts the chip-export failures in front of Lutnick days before he flies with Trump.

China’s Foreign Ministry said Monday the three leaders will meet Thursday in Shenzhen, on the sidelines of APEC. Spokeswoman Mao Ning called it the first face-to-face meeting of the three heads of state and said it had “groundbreaking significance” (NBC News). The White House says Trump will raise cutting nuclear arsenals and the Iran war, not Ukraine (CNBC). A White House official told NPR there is no expected deal on nuclear arms, calling the meeting an “entry point,” and that no separate Trump–Putin meeting is planned. Trump stops first at the ASEAN summit in Manila, and Elon Musk will join him in Shenzhen (NPR). Semafor reports Lutnick is expected on Air Force One, with Bessent also expected on the trip, and a senior official says the Nvidia scandal “should not really have any bearing” on it (Semafor).

Why it matters to you. Chip-export policy is the hinge for AI capex and for Nvidia’s China revenue, and it is now a money-and-influence fight too. Cantwell, the top Democrat on Senate Commerce, wrote that Commerce “resisted efforts to restrict companies flagged as national security threats.” She noted that Cantor Fitzgerald, now run by Lutnick’s sons, held $1.2 billion in Nvidia stock when he was nominated. She also said the administration’s 25% fee on some Nvidia chip sales to China creates “a perverse financial incentive” to approve exports. Separately, Sens. Elizabeth Warren and Chris Coons gave Lutnick, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and chief of staff Stephen Miller until Tuesday, Nov. 17, to confirm they have ordered Nvidia-export records preserved (CNBC). In the House, incoming Foreign Affairs chair Gregory Meeks is floating a chips-and-AI subpanel as committees fight over who leads (Semafor). The Journal reports the administration will name Nvidia among AI “national champions” for overseas sales (WSJ, headline only). We could not confirm whether Commerce has answered Cantwell. Why: CNBC said the department had not responded Monday, and we found no statement.

Divergence — summit of substance, or photo op?

What is in dispute. A dispute over interpretation. Beijing frames the meeting as historic. Strategist David Roche told CNBC it is an “almost 100% photo op” and that China will not join arms talks until it reaches parity, which he puts about 10 years off. Former ambassador Edgard Kagan told NPR that Beijing fears being locked into a precedent. On chips, the White House says Nvidia will not come up; Cantwell and Warren are pressing for the opposite.

Why the vantage differs. China gains standing simply by hosting. The White House wants a nuclear headline and to keep the Nvidia scandal off the table. Cantwell and Warren, soon in the majority, gain from tying Lutnick to the scandal before he testifies to Senate Banking in December. The analysts quoted advise investors and are paid to discount the pageantry.

What it means for the reader. Judge Thursday by what is written down: any joint statement, any word on chip licenses or the 25% fee, and whether Tuesday’s preservation deadline is met. Pictures alone change nothing for AI supply chains.

A Texas U.S. attorney warns Sen. Elizabeth Warren, the likely next Banking Committee chair, of “significant legal consequences” and gives her until Nov. 23 to preserve records on probes of Trump associates

Broader domestic institutions Core — US financials Continuation Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 3 newsrooms (NBC News, Fox News, Semafor), plus a Guardian live-blog headline as signal.

Continuation trigger: material connection and threshold event. Aaron Reitz’s campaign of letters, which began with Sen. Richard Blumenthal on Nov. 11, now reaches the senator expected to oversee the big banks, with a dated deadline.

Reitz, the U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas and a former chief of staff to Sen. Ted Cruz, wrote that Warren seems “eager to wield your future gavel like a tomahawk, hunting for the scalps of President Trump’s private associates.” The letter is full of jabs at her past claims of Native American ancestry (NBC News). It cites the conspiracy-against-rights and color-of-law statutes, 18 U.S.C. §§ 241 and 242. It demands written confirmation by 6 p.m. ET on Monday, Nov. 23, that she is preserving records. Failure “may carry significant legal consequences” (Fox News). Reitz posted the letter on X and Trump reposted it. Reitz has sent similar letters to Blumenthal and Sen. Elissa Slotkin. Warren’s office did not respond Sunday night (NBC News).

Why it matters to you. Warren on Face the Nation said tech CEOs, Wall Street executives and giant corporations that broke the law “better get ready for some investigations,” and Reitz quoted that back at her. If she takes the Banking gavel in January, JPMorgan and its peers, crypto firms and AI-in-finance deals are in her lane. Democrats have named Trump family businesses, corporate corruption, immigration enforcement and the Epstein case as their first targets (NBC News). Semafor reports they also plan probes of Africa minerals deals (Semafor, headline only). On Nov. 11, NBC reported that a step like this against a sitting member would normally go through DOJ’s Public Integrity Section, which has been shrunk (NBC News). We could not confirm how Warren will answer or whether DOJ leadership approved this letter. Why: neither had said so publicly by Monday morning.

Divergence — protecting citizens, or deterring oversight?

What is in dispute. A legal and constitutional dispute. Reitz says the senators’ document demands to private donors chill First Amendment association rights. Acting Deputy Attorney General Trent McCotter has backed him, saying DOJ “will defend against threats to those rights.” Blumenthal says DOJ is “weaponizing” itself to target Congress. Fox News notes that the letter does not establish Warren broke any law, and that legitimate congressional investigations have constitutional protection.

Why the vantage differs. Reitz serves at the president’s pleasure, ran unsuccessfully for Texas attorney general, and posts his letters publicly, so they work partly as signals to Trump’s base. Warren and Blumenthal are about to gain subpoena power and gain from being seen as targets. NBC’s point about Public Integrity is a structural observation, not a ruling. Fox’s caveat comes from its legal desk.

What it means for the reader. A preservation letter is not a charge. The thresholds to watch are Nov. 23 and any grand-jury subpoena or court filing naming a senator. Short of that, this is a fight over what Wall Street and tech should expect from a Democratic Banking Committee.

Dig deeper: the letters so far

Wednesday, Nov. 11: Warren, Blumenthal and Rep. Robert Garcia ask Trump fundraiser and National Park Foundation board member Meredith O’Rourke to keep records on ballroom funding. The same day, Reitz orders Blumenthal to preserve records and the White House warns Democrats that probes could be a crime. Sen. Elissa Slotkin has received a similar letter; NBC does not give its date. Sunday: Warren, with the Nov. 23 deadline. Blumenthal answered with a preservation demand of his own to Reitz (NBC News, Nov. 11 and Nov. 16).

An Army major tells Congress the Kuwait post where Iran killed six soldiers on March 1 had no counter-drone defense and no doctor; Gold Star families say the Army has admitted no mistakes

Geopolitics Broader domestic institutions New Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 2 newsrooms (CBS News and Time), plus an AP report relayed by Time. CBS has led the reporting since March.

Maj. Stephen Ramsbottom, wounded in the attack at Port Shuaiba, filed a whistleblower complaint on Friday with the House and Senate Armed Services committees. He says he twice raised the lack of protection from the air and was told, “We don’t have to worry about protection.” He says a requested counter-drone system “never arrived,” and that the post had “no physician, no fixed aid station, and only one ambulance.” His lawyers want an independent investigation (Time). On Sunday’s 60 Minutes, six soldiers said they had seen intelligence before the war that put Port Shuaiba on an Iranian target list. Weeks before the war, the Pentagon moved 90% of its ground forces out of Kuwait, but about 70 members of the 103rd Sustainment Command were sent to the port, about 65 miles from Iran. Joey Amor, whose wife, Master Sgt. Nicole Amor, was killed, said of the command: “They killed my wife.” The Pentagon declined to say whether the unit was denied adequate force protection, and two generals named by soldiers did not respond (CBS News).

Why it matters to you. This is the war’s deadliest single attack on U.S. troops, and 23 service members have now died in eight months (Time). The complaint lands as Congress argues over funding the war (see the Dec. 11 item below) and as Iran keeps launching drones at U.S. sites in the Gulf. Whether commanders ignored warnings is an accountability question for the Pentagon. It is also a test of whether Gulf bases are now defended against cheap drones. Time’s headline says Democrats are calling for a probe of military leaders; we read only the part of the story about the complaint.

Divergence — what the Army has told families, and when

What is in dispute. Facts and timing. On March 2, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth called the drone a “squirter” that slipped through to a “fortified” operations center. Pentagon spokesman Sean Parnell has said the site had “6-foot walls” (CBS News, July). Soldiers say it had walls against car bombs and nothing against drones. On timing, CBS reported in July that the Army finished its investigation in June and privately briefed families on July 9; Time cites AP to the same effect. Sunday’s 60 Minutes says families “are finally being briefed” this week, and one widow said she had been briefed “yesterday.”

Why the vantage differs. Hegseth and Parnell speak for the leadership whose decisions are in question. Ramsbottom was wounded there and accepts part of the blame himself. The families have seen the findings but not their public release. CBS’s July story and its broadcast were reported months apart, which may explain the timing gap.

What it means for the reader. We could not confirm whether there is a second round of briefings this week or whether the broadcast line reflects earlier taping. Why: the Army has not said, and the findings remain unpublished. The concrete next steps are any Armed Services inquiry and any public release of the June report.

A U.S. official told ABC the USS Theodore Roosevelt strike group entered Central Command’s area Sunday, joining two other carriers among more than 20 Navy ships enforcing the blockade of Iranian ports (ABC News). Iran says there will be no U.N. inspections of its damaged nuclear sites until the war is “permanently ended.” Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi claims a 10-nation regional bloc backs his plan to reopen the strait within two days of a ceasefire; the other members have not confirmed that, and Washington calls Iran’s conditions “unacceptable” (CBS News). At home, AAA says Florida’s average was $3.62 a gallon on Sunday, down 61 cents in a month and 91 cents below this year’s high, as the IEA reports global supply now exceeds demand (Fox 13). Why it matters: so far the price at the pump is following global supply, not the daily attacks. Al Jazeera’s live-blog headline says Iran “commandeered” a U.S. surveillance drone; we could not read the blog’s text, so we cannot confirm it (Al Jazeera, headline).

Divergence — mine or missile?

What is in dispute. A factual dispute. The Revolutionary Guard navy, via state news agency IRNA, says a tanker hit naval mines east of the strait on Sunday after switching off its navigation and entering a “restricted zone.” It offered no images. UKMTO’s Monday report says an “unknown projectile.” Neither names the ship, so it is unclear whether these are the same incident.

Why the vantage differs. Iran wants to enforce its claimed control of the strait while denying that it attacks ships. UKMTO relays what ship operators report, quickly and without attribution.

What it means for the reader. Treat both as one-sided until the vessel and its flag are named. The pump price is the number to watch for Florida, and so far it is falling.
