Alaska and Mississippi harden into affidavit fights and a lawsuit over rejected absentees

Elections Follow-up Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 4. Trigger: new litigation steps and county-level affidavit tallies since morning.

Chamber control is settled; the size of the Democratic Senate majority is not. USA Today reports Scott Colom leads Cindy Hyde-Smith by roughly 2,500 votes with affidavit (provisional) ballots still being cured through 10 November and county reports due 13 November. Mississippi has no statewide recount statute. Hyde-Smith’s campaign and the NRSC have asked all 82 counties to set affidavit ballots aside — prelude to scattered county suits — while Colom’s lawyers say pulling already-processed ballots would itself violate state law.

The Mississippi Free Press finds Colom overperforming among accepted affidavits even in Hyde-Smith counties (Chickasaw affidavits 77.1% Colom). In Alaska, Mary Peltola leads Dan Sullivan by 637 with about 74% in; ranked-choice tabulation is set for 18 November. On 6 November Peltola’s side sued for names of voters whose absentees were rejected; the state refused, citing confidentiality. Election Central notes a Democratic sweep of both would make the chamber 56–44; both Republican holds would leave 54–46.

What to watch: Mississippi affidavit accept/reject rates by county; Alaska Division counts on 10 and 13 November; whether any county court enjoins counting.

Divergence — ordinary curing, or a manufactured contest?

What is in dispute. Significance dispute more than facts: both sides accept the published margins; they disagree on whether affidavit volume and the other Dan Sullivan on Alaska’s ballot justify extraordinary process.

Why the vantage differs. Party lawyers optimise for courthouse options before certification. Local reporting (Free Press) is measuring ballot-type splits the national desks summarise. Timing: morning tallies were static; Saturday added litigation posture.

What it means for the reader. Price the Senate at 54-plus Democrats. Treat both races as margin fights, not chamber flips. The observation that settles Mississippi is the 13 November county packages; for Alaska, the 18 November ranked-choice run.

Homelessness fell on the January count — HUD and the Alliance disagree on why

Domestic institutions New subject Independent corroboration

Independent accounts compared: 3. Negative-check / underreported lead.

HUD says the January 2026 Point-in-Time count found 698,425 people homeless on a single night — a 6.3% drop since January 2025 and, in the department’s framing, the largest decrease on record — and credits border enforcement plus a shift from Housing First to “Treatment First.” The National Alliance to End Homelessness independently estimates a 6.7% drop (to about 693,000), notes decreases began before the current administration, and says HUD issued a hastily drafted Friday release without subpopulation data after learning the Alliance would publish. IBTimes covers both claims.

What to watch: HUD’s full CoC tables; chronic homelessness (Alliance flags a 1% rise); whether Congress funds Treatment First grants or Housing First restorations.

Divergence — who gets credit for the drop?

What is in dispute. Facts dispute on exact national totals (698k vs ~693k) and a significance dispute on causation (border/Treatment First vs provider-system momentum that predated 2025).

Why the vantage differs. HUD is an administration press release with policy branding. The Alliance is an advocacy research shop publishing a modeled CoC sample and contesting the credit narrative. Timing: both dropped around 6 November; HUD’s statement arrived after the Alliance signalled publication.

What it means for the reader. Treat the direction (down, second year) as solid; treat the causal story as unsettled until full HUD microdata and independent replication land. Chronic homelessness ticking up is the counter-signal inside the same Alliance release.
